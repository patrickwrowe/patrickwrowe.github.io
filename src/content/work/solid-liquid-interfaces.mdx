How water behaves against a surface governs friction, flow, wetting and nucleation at the
nanoscale. It is also the regime where the usual tools give out: continuum hydrodynamics
stops applying somewhere around a few molecular diameters, and first-principles dynamics
cannot reach the length and time scales the questions are asked at. Two-dimensional materials
sharpen the problem, because in a nanotube or a laminate gallery the interface is essentially
all there is.

This is a thread rather than a single paper. It ran through the back half of my time at UCL,
in Angelos Michaelides' group, and it has two halves that fed each other.

## Making interface models cheap

The methodological half is
[machine learning potentials for complex aqueous systems made simple](/work/aqueous-potentials/).
The argument there is that most interfacial questions do not need a general model of an
element's whole chemistry. They need a model that is right for one system at one state point,
and such a model can be built in days from a short ab initio trajectory, with no per-system
tuning and a committee of networks reporting its own uncertainty as it runs.

Six deliberately dissimilar systems went through the same unchanged protocol: ions in
solution, water in carbon and boron nitride nanotubes, water between MoS₂ sheets, and water on
rutile TiO₂.

## Spending them

The other half is what the models were for.

[Water flow in single-wall nanotubes](/work/nanotube-water-flow/) is the clearest payoff. Two
of the six demonstration systems above stopped being demonstrations and became the object of
study, over forty nanoseconds across sixteen tube radii, which is what it took to separate
the geometric part of nanotube friction from the chemical part.

Alongside that ran the collaborations with Rahul Nair's experimental group at the National
Graphene Institute in Manchester, on
[vermiculite membranes](/work/vermiculite-membranes/) and later
[MoS₂ membranes](/work/mos2-membranes/). Those followed a consistent pattern: the
experimentalists had a clean anomaly they could measure and could not explain, and wanted a
mechanism rather than a curve fit. Both ended up hinging on the structure of a nanometre or
two of confined water.

## What I took from it

Two things, and neither is about water.

The first is that the useful unit of work here was a protocol rather than a model. A model
someone else has to refit before they can use it does not travel; a procedure that reliably
produces a working model in a few days does.

The second is that the hardest part of an interdisciplinary collaboration is agreeing what
question the simulation is answering. The MoS₂ work is the good example: the informative
result was a *negative* one, that lithium loading does not change how fast water diffuses in
an open channel, which only counts as an answer because the question had been sharpened
enough beforehand for a negative to eliminate something.
