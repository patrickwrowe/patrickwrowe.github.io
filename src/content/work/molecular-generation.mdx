This is a side project, and it exists because SMILES is a genuinely odd thing to hand a
language model. A SMILES string looks like text and behaves like a serialised graph. It has a
grammar with long-range dependencies — every open bracket needs its close, every ring-opening
digit needs its partner, possibly thirty characters later — and unlike English, a string that
gets the grammar slightly wrong does not read awkwardly, it denotes nothing at all.

So there is a clean question underneath: what does a small model have to learn before it can
produce a molecule, and what does it get wrong on the way?

## What it does

Character-level recurrent models over SMILES, written in PyTorch and trained from scratch
rather than fine-tuned from anything. A plain RNN and an LSTM to establish the floor, then the
model the project is actually about: an LSTM whose initial hidden and cell states are produced
from an indication vector, so generation can be conditioned on what the molecule is supposed
to treat.

The training data is ChEMBL, restricted to molecules that reached phase 4 or 5 of clinical
development, labelled with their MeSH indications and cut to the fifty most common. Ask for an
antineoplastic and the model is being steered towards that region of chemical space by its own
starting state, rather than by filtering the output afterwards.

The tokeniser is trained on the same corpus and committed alongside the code, since
character-level, atom-level and learned-subword vocabularies each break differently on ring
closures.

## What it gets wrong

A small LSTM trained this way does learn valid SMILES, which means it has implicitly picked up
atom valences, common functional groups and the bracket-matching rules of the notation. That
is more than it sounds like.

The failures split cleanly in two, and the split is the useful output:

**Syntactic** — the string is not valid SMILES. Usually an unclosed parenthesis or a ring
digit opened and never closed, both of which are exactly the long-range dependencies a
character-level recurrent model is worst at.

**Semantic** — the string parses but the molecule cannot exist, typically an atom given more
bonds than it has. Fluorine with two of them.

There is also a persistent bias towards long saturated hydrocarbon chains, which is
straightforwardly the model hedging: if you have no idea what comes next, another carbon is
rarely locally wrong.

This is why a bare validity percentage is a weak metric. Two models at the same validity rate
can be wrong in these two quite different ways, and only one of them gets better with scale in
the way you would hope. There is more on this in
[SMILES is a strange language to model](/writing/smiles-is-a-strange-language/).

## Status

Active, occasionally, on evenings. The code and the notebooks are on GitHub; the notebooks are
the readable entry point, since each one carries its own outputs and figures. Transformer
