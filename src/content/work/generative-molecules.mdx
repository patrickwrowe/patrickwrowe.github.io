A molecule can be written as a line of text. In SMILES, the line notation that cheminformatics uses for chemical structures, caffeine (a molecule most readers will have within reach) is `CN1C=NC2=C1C(=O)N(C(=O)N2C)C`: atoms as letters, bonds as the order the letters come in, a branch inside parentheses, and a ring opened by a digit and closed, some way further along, by the same digit again. It looks like a language, and it is tempting to treat it as one, with a grammar and a vocabulary of its own. The difference from English is in what a near miss costs. A sentence with one wrong character reads awkwardly and means what it meant. A SMILES string with one parenthesis left open, or a ring digit that never finds its partner, is not an awkward molecule. It is nothing at all, and its reader, which is a parser, says so.

That asymmetry is the subject of this page, and the two projects on it are the same experiment run in the two languages. Both are my own, written from scratch in PyTorch in 2025 to understand the machinery rather than to beat a benchmark. {/* Q: 7 */} Nothing here was fine-tuned from a pretrained model, and nothing here is benchmarked for novelty or for whether a chemist could make what comes out. The first project is a character-level long short-term memory network (LSTM, a recurrent network that reads a sequence one character at a time and carries a memory along with it) trained on drug molecules from ChEMBL, with the condition each drug treats handed to the model as its starting state. The second is the same class of model pointed at the complete works of Shakespeare, where a near miss is forgiven, and it serves as the control. The short version of what they found: a 4-layer LSTM of 256 hidden units, trained for about 22 minutes on one machine, learns enough SMILES grammar that its mistakes separate into two kinds, strings that are not valid notation and strings that are valid notation for a molecule that cannot exist. That split, more than any molecule it produced, is the result worth keeping: it is the reason a single validity rate is a weak way to compare two generative models.

## ChEMBL generation

The README of this project warns, in its first line, that it is out of date almost as soon as it is written, and I will take it at its word: everything below describes one run, from one checkpoint, and the repository's 135 commits run from July to November 2025. {/* Q: 6 */} The question it was built around is narrower than whether a language model can make molecules. It is what a small model has to learn before it can emit one, and whether the thing the molecule is meant to treat can go in as the model's starting state rather than as a sieve run over its output afterwards.

The molecules come from ChEMBL 35, a release of the database whose tables hold molecules, the conditions each has been trialled against and how far each trial got; they were read from a local SQLite copy with one SQL join across four of its tables. I kept every row whose indication had reached clinical phase 3 or beyond. {/* Q: 4 */} Each indication is a Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) term, one-hot encoded (one column per indication, with a 1 in the column that applies), and any indication carrying fewer than 50 molecules is pooled into a single Other column, which leaves about 80 named indications and one bin for the rest. {/* Q: 1 */} The split is 80% for training and 20% for validation. What the repository does not print anywhere I could read is the size of the training set or the exact number of indications, so I will not give either. {/* Q: 1 */}

The shape of that set is the awkward part. Other is the largest bar; Neoplasms is the largest named indication, then Breast Neoplasms, Depressive Disorder and Cardiovascular Diseases, and from there a long tail runs down through Pain, Infections, Bacterial Infections, Schizophrenia and Hypertension to Acne Vulgaris, flattening out at the 50-molecule floor that the pooling rule sets. {/* Q: 1 */} The conditioning signal, therefore, is far stronger for some labels than for others, and any honest reading of what the model does has to be made one indication at a time rather than in aggregate.

The tokeniser is as plain as it can be made: character level, with a vocabulary that is the sorted set of characters in the corpus plus 3 reserved tokens, a pad, a beginning-of-string `<` and an end-of-string `>`. The model is an LSTM of 4 layers and 256 hidden units, with dropout of 0.4 (the fraction of units switched off at random during training) on the output, on the recurrent connections and on the initial state. The conditioning is the part the project is about. The indication, one-hot and reduced to an integer, passes through two learned lookup tables, one for the hidden state and one for the cell state (the two pieces of memory an LSTM carries), and what comes out is the state every layer starts from. The model is told what the drug is for before it reads its first character. Ask for Neoplasms and it begins in that region of chemical space; nothing is filtered afterwards.

Training ran for 64 epochs at about 21 s each, about 22 minutes in all, with a learning rate of 10⁻³, weight decay of 10⁻⁵, batches of 128 molecules and gradient norms clipped at 10.0. The loss fell from 0.8586 on the training set and 0.8678 on the validation set after the first epoch to 0.0656 and 0.1277 after the last; the validation loss was lowest at epoch 63, at 0.1249, and had turned up by epoch 64. The checkpoint that everything below comes from was written on 7 August 2025.

What comes out is sampled 25 at a time, in a grid of 5 by 5, each string capped at 100 characters, and each cell redrawn up to 10 times until RDKit (the open-source cheminformatics toolkit that parses the strings) accepts one. Prompted with nothing but the beginning-of-string character and the Bacterial Infections label at temperature 0.9 (the sampling parameter: higher is more random), the 25 molecules read, by the README's own account, as macrocyclic, anthracycline-like and β-lactam-like antibiotics. Prompted with a fragment of sulfamethoxazole, `CC1=CC(=NO1)NS(=O)(=O)C`, and the Cardiovascular Diseases label at temperature 0.7, every one of the 25 keeps the isoxazole–sulfonamide core it was handed and varies the rest; at temperature 1.3 with the same prompt the set spreads out. That is the behaviour to want from a model like this if it is to be useful for anything: hold the part a chemist has already decided on, and explore around it. Two of the strings I kept, in a commented-out cell of the README, are a triple macrocycle that looks, by eye, like a glycopeptide antibiotic of the vancomycin family, and a cyclic polypeptide closed by a disulfide bridge; neither has a recorded indication, so I will not assign one. {/* Q: 2 */}

A grid is a picture of the successes, and I want to be plain about what it cannot show. Because each cell is redrawn until a string parses, no validity rate can be read from one, and the repository holds no validity rate for the conditioned model at all. The only count of that kind in it comes from an earlier, unconditioned version of the project, in which a plain recurrent neural network (RNN) completed a prompt built around caffeine at temperature 0.8: 10 of 20 strings parsed, and the README now marks that route as superseded. The grids also show that valid is not the same as useful. Of the 25 Bacterial Infections samples, at least 3 are nothing but a sodium ion and a chloride ion, which parse perfectly because ChEMBL's canonical SMILES carry their counter-ions (the ions that balance a drug supplied as a salt), and several more are near-duplicates of one four-ring anthracycline-like system or of one steroid-like scaffold. {/* Q: 2 */} Every one of those counts as a valid molecule, and the salt at least is easy to make.

The failures are the useful output, and they separate cleanly into two kinds. A syntactic failure is a string that is not SMILES: a parenthesis opened and never closed, or a ring digit with no partner. In the sample on record, RDKit gave up around character 96, at an unclosed parenthesis. A semantic failure is a string that parses and denotes a molecule that cannot exist, an atom given more bonds than it can carry; the one on record is a fluorine given 2. The README pairs that message with a string that contains no fluorine, so the message and the string come from different samples, and I give you only the message. Both kinds come – I think – from the long-range dependencies that a character-level recurrent model is worst at. There is a third habit that is not a failure of either kind: a bias towards long saturated hydrocarbon chains, which is the model hedging. When it has no idea what comes next, another carbon is rarely wrong locally. In fairness to the model, the training data contains some of those chains, so it was not inventing them.

That is why a bare validity rate is a weak metric, and why it is a tempting one: it is the number a parser hands over for free. Two models at the same validity rate can be wrong in these two different ways, and the README's own experience is that the syntactic kind shrinks with longer training and a larger model, which says nothing about the other kind. Transformer variants and a benchmark for novelty are the obvious next steps, and as of the last commit, on 25 November 2025, neither is done. {/* Q: 6 */} A validity rate tells you how often the parser said yes. The reason it said no is the part that teaches you anything.

## Infinite monkeys

This is the same machinery pointed at a language that forgives. In the commit record the molecule project came first: the Shakespeare repository was cut from it on 21 July 2025, in 5 commits made that day, and it carries the same scaffolding of modules, training loop and data code. {/* Q: 3 */} Its README opens on Andrej Karpathy's verdict that recurrent networks are unreasonably effective, and the notebooks are a test of that on one text, with no help given.

The help withheld is the point. A character-level model is told nothing about the language: not that words exist, or spaces, or that a capitalised name followed by a full stop means somebody is about to speak. Whatever structure it produces, it worked out from the stream of characters, and the exercise is watching where that runs out. The corpus is the complete works of Shakespeare as a single text file, read character by character into sequences of 32 characters over a vocabulary of 84. The model is an LSTM of 512 hidden units and 3 layers, trained with a learning rate of 10⁻³, weight decay of 10⁻⁴, batches of 128 sequences and gradient norms clipped at 3.0, configured for 64 epochs. The sample below is the README's, drawn at epoch 30 of those 64 at temperature 0.9 with the prompt `AUGUSTUS.`, and it is quoted from its first line, which begins with the prompt itself.

```text
AUGUSTUS. For so do I about
    that fair is time not Guildenster.
  Pedro. It, protest you, and such a plighted speeding too.
    But let me ripht from honesty to for his grape. When you may redeem us
```

It has the shape of the thing. Speaker labels in capitals with a full stop after them, a second speaker brought in on an indented line, verse set in from the margin, a cadence that could be read aloud and, elsewhere in its output, stage directions: none of it asked for, all of it inferred. Read it twice and there is nothing underneath. Guildenster is Guildenstern with the last letter dropped, and `ripht` for `right` is the spelling mechanism showing through: the next character is whatever usually follows the last few, and nothing in the model checks that the result is a word. The syntax is plausible, and the meaning never arrives. It is also a mid-training draw, from epoch 30 of a 64-epoch configuration, not a final model. The README states the limit itself, and so will I: I am not putting any playwrights out of work.

That gap between looks right and is right is the whole reason to build these, and it is the gap the first half of this page ran into in the other language. Shakespeare forgives a near miss; a human reader fills in `right` and moves on. SMILES does not, because its reader is a parser, and a string with one bracket out of place is refused rather than misread. Chemistry is the less forgiving case, and that is exactly what makes it the more informative one: the parser's refusals are what let the molecule project sort its failures into two kinds, and nothing in a page of pastiche sorts itself. For the record, a third repository, pytorch-character-level-language-models, holds a names classifier notebook, 7 commits from 20 July 2025, the day before the split, behind an empty README; it is the classification side of the same idea, as far as a filename can say so. {/* Q: 5 */} If you want to know what a model has learned, hand it a language that refuses a near miss.
