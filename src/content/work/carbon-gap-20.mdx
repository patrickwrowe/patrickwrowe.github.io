Carbon is several materials wearing one element. Diamond, graphite, graphene, nanotubes,
fullerenes, amorphous films and hot liquid differ enough in their bonding that a potential
fitted to any one of them is usually wrong about the rest. The
[graphene potential](/work/graphene-potential/) I had developed two years earlier is a clean
illustration: twenty times more accurate than any empirical alternative for graphene, and
constitutionally incapable of describing diamond.

The obvious question is whether the accuracy and the coverage are genuinely in tension, or
whether the tension is an artefact of how these models had been built. GAP-20 is the attempt
to have both in a single model, and to say precisely what having both costs.

## What we built

A Gaussian approximation potential trained on plane-wave DFT with the dispersion-inclusive
optB88-vdW functional, over a final training set of 6,088 configurations. It merges two
existing databases, the amorphous-carbon set behind GAP-17 and the graphene set from the 2018
paper, and extends them with point defects, surfaces, nanotubes and other nanostructures. Every configuration was recomputed at
one consistent level of theory regardless of where it came from. That recomputation is
unglamorous and it is what makes merging two independently constructed databases defensible
at all; without it the model would be fitting the difference between two functionals as if it
were physics.

Two pieces of model engineering carry most of the weight.

The first is a semi-analytical spline fitted to an r⁻⁶ decay, added to the two-body term. The
SOAP descriptor has a finite cutoff and simply cannot see interlayer distances, so without an
explicit long-range term the layers in graphite do not attract one another and the whole
structure is unbound. Dispersion is not a correction here; it is the interaction holding half
the phase diagram together.

The second is a systematic scan of the SOAP hyperparameters, cutoff radius and number of
sparse points and basis order, against force error on a held-out set, rather than inheriting
the defaults that had accumulated in the literature. We settled on a 4.5 Å cutoff and 9000 sparse
points. The force error converges by roughly 1500 sparse points, so 9000 is well past
saturation, but the additional evaluation cost is negligible and the margin is cheap
insurance against configurations the scan did not cover.

## What it showed

Lattice parameters and bond lengths across every crystalline allotrope tested come out to
**0.2%** average error, and formation energies to **0.5%**. For comparison on lattice
parameters: Tersoff 5%, REBO-II 4%, AIREBO 1%, LCBOP 0.3%.

Defects and surfaces are the harder test, because both are exactly the local environments a
training set drawn from bulk phases under-samples. Defect formation energies land mostly
within 10%, and relaxed defect geometries agree with DFT to better than 10⁻² Å RMSD in all but
a handful of cases. Stone–Wales defects in graphene and graphite carry no error at the
precision quoted. Diamond defects are the clear weak spot at 25–35%. Diamond surface energies
are typically within 7%, with the relaxed (111) reconstruction the worst case at 15%.

The strongest evidence in the paper is the transferability test, because none of the
structures involved appear anywhere in the training set. We ran a random structure search:
1000 random eight-atom configurations relaxed under GAP-20, then recomputed with DFT. The
energy distributions agree, and the search recovers AB- and AA-stacked graphite, cubic and
hexagonal diamond, haeckelite, a crosslinked graphitic phase, and an sp¹-rich structure that
nothing in the setup was designed to find.

Liquid carbon matches DFT across 5000–9500 K and 1.5–3.5 g cm⁻³, in both radial and angular
distribution functions, including the rise in sp¹ fraction at low density that shows up as
bond angles approaching 180°.

## Where this sits

The model merges two lineages: the GAP-17 amorphous-carbon database of Deringer and Csányi,
and my own [graphene potential](/work/graphene-potential/). The abstract frames it exactly
that way, as combining the flexibility needed for amorphous carbon with the numerical accuracy
needed for crystalline graphene.

The cost of doing so is quantified rather than hidden. Phonon frequencies at high-symmetry
points are correct to 4 meV, against 1 meV for the dedicated graphene model: a factor of
four, traded for diamond, nanotubes, defects, surfaces and the amorphous phase. On the
evidence of the random structure search, that is a good trade.

The following year I co-authored [a paper](/work/aqueous-potentials/) making close to the
opposite argument, which cites GAP-20 as an example of the years-long, general-purpose model
it proposes an alternative to. The two positions are not in conflict. They answer different
questions about what a potential is for.

It is the most-used thing I have built, at around 360 citations as of mid-2026.

## Getting it

[The repository](https://github.com/patrickwrowe/Carbon_GAP) holds the fitted potential as a
QUIP file that runs directly in LAMMPS, the complete 6,088-configuration training set as an
extended XYZ, and a worked example job. Nothing needs refitting before it can be used. The
same artefacts are archived with a DOI through the
[Cambridge repository](https://doi.org/10.17863/CAM.54529), and both the model and the data
are CC BY 4.0.

Publishing the artefact rather than the recipe was deliberate. A potential distributed as a
methodology section is a potential nobody outside the group ever runs, and for this kind of
work the useful unit of publication is the thing itself. Of the two halves, the training set
is arguably the more valuable: it took far longer to assemble than the fit did, and it is what
someone building a different carbon model would actually want.

Running it needs QUIP and GAP, whose
[installation instructions](https://libatoms.github.io/GAP/installation.html) are upstream;
those carry their own licence, separate from the CC BY on the model.
