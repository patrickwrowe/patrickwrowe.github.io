Carbon is several materials wearing one element. Diamond, graphite, graphene, nanotubes,
fullerenes, amorphous films, hot liquid — the bonding differs enough between them that a
potential fitted to one is usually wrong about the others. My
[graphene potential](/work/graphene-potential/) from two years earlier was a clean example:
excellent on the thing it was trained on, useless on diamond.

GAP-20 is the attempt to have both.

## What we built

A Gaussian approximation potential trained on plane-wave DFT with the dispersion-inclusive
optB88-vdW functional. The training set merges two existing databases — the amorphous-carbon
set behind GAP-17 and the graphene set from my 2018 paper — and extends them with defects,
surfaces, nanotubes and other nanostructures. Everything was recomputed at one consistent
level of theory regardless of where it came from, which is what makes merging databases
legitimate rather than convenient.

Two pieces of model engineering carry most of the weight:

- A semi-analytical spline fitted to an r⁻⁶ decay, added to the two-body term. The SOAP
  descriptor has a finite cutoff and cannot reach interlayer distances, so without this
  graphite layers do not attract each other.
- A systematic scan of the SOAP hyperparameters — cutoff, sparse points, basis order — against
  force error on a held-out set, rather than the usual inherited defaults. Settled at a 4.5 Å
  cutoff and 9000 sparse points, well past the ~1500 where the error converges.

## What it showed

Lattice parameters and bond lengths across every crystalline allotrope tested come out to
**0.2%** average error, formation energies to **0.5%**. Tersoff manages 5% on lattice
parameters, REBO-II 4%, AIREBO 1%.

Where it is weaker is worth saying. Defect formation energies land mostly within 10%, but
diamond defects are the weak spot at 25–35%. The relaxed diamond (111) surface energy is 15%
out. Stone–Wales defects in graphene and graphite carry no error to the precision quoted.

The transferability test is the result I find most convincing, because nothing in it was
trained on. We ran a random structure search — 1000 random eight-atom configurations relaxed
under GAP-20, then recomputed with DFT. The energies agree, and the search recovers AB- and
AA-stacked graphite, cubic and hexagonal diamond, haeckelite, a crosslinked graphitic phase
and an sp¹-rich structure we had not gone looking for.

Liquid carbon matches DFT across 5000–9500 K and 1.5–3.5 g cm⁻³, including the rise in sp¹
fraction at low density.

## Where this sits

It merges two lineages: the GAP-17 amorphous-carbon database of Deringer and Csányi, and my
own [graphene potential](/work/graphene-potential/). The framing in the abstract is exactly
that — combining the flexibility needed for amorphous carbon with the numerical accuracy
needed for crystalline graphene.

The cost of doing so is quantified rather than hidden: phonon frequencies at high-symmetry
points correct to 4 meV, against 1 meV for the dedicated graphene model. A factor of four,
traded for diamond, nanotubes, defects, surfaces and the amorphous phase.

The potential and its training configurations are published under CC BY 4.0 through the
Cambridge repository, packaged as a QUIP file that runs in LAMMPS. That was deliberate — the
point of a general potential is that other groups use it.

The following year I was a co-author on
[a paper arguing against this whole approach](/work/aqueous-potentials/), which cites GAP-20
as an example of the years-long, general-purpose model it proposes an alternative to. Both
positions are right, for different questions.
