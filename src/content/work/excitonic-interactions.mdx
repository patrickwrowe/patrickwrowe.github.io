When an excited state moves from one molecule to another, the rate depends on the excitonic
coupling between them. Essentially every model of energy transfer takes that coupling from a
Coulombic calculation, whether the subject is photosynthesis, an organic solar cell or a
light-emitting film. Often from the crudest version of one: treat each chromophore's transition
density as a point dipole, and compute the interaction between two dipoles.

That approximation has a clear physical justification, which is that it becomes exact as the
molecules move apart. The question this paper asks is what it costs when they do not move
apart, when chromophores are stacked in contact, as they are in a pigment–protein complex and
in every molecular semiconductor.

The answer is that it costs most of the coupling.

## What was done

Excitonic couplings computed two ways for the same chromophore pairs, so that the difference
between them isolates exactly the term the standard approach discards.

The Coulombic coupling came from transition density cubes: the full electrostatic interaction
between the two transition densities, without a multipole expansion. The *total*
coupling came from a diabatisation scheme based on Mulliken population analysis, which
retains everything: electrostatics plus the short-range terms that only exist where the two
molecular wavefunctions actually overlap. Subtract one from the other and what remains is the
short-range contribution, which is exchange, orbital overlap and penetration.

Everything ran at TDDFT with the LC-ωPBE range-separated functional and a 6-31G** basis in
NWChem, on homodimers only, with phytyl tails cut back to methyl groups so that the
calculation is about the chromophore rather than its solubilising chemistry.

Five systems, chosen to span the two fields that use these couplings and rarely talk to each
other. Three light-harvesting complexes: the Fenna–Matthews–Olson complex, the LH2 antenna
ring, and peridinin–chlorophyll protein, each taken from its crystal structure. Two
phthalocyanine solids: a crystalline octabutoxy phthalocyanine and an amorphous zinc
phthalocyanine, both sampled over 250 snapshots from molecular dynamics rather than evaluated
on a single idealised geometry, because in a real film the coupling is a distribution and not
a number.

## What it showed

The size of the neglected term scales with contact, and the two families of system sit at
opposite ends of that scale.

In the light-harvesting complexes the effect is real but contained. The most strongly
interacting bacteriochlorophyll dimers in FMO carry a short-range contribution of about 28%,
which in absolute terms is 4 meV. In the LH2 B850 ring, where the pigments are packed
considerably closer, neighbouring bacteriochlorophylls reach 66%, around 3 meV out of 5. In
peridinin–chlorophyll protein the peridinin dimers come in at 22% and 10%.

In the molecular semiconductors it is not contained at all. Face-to-face dimers in
crystalline octabutoxy phthalocyanine show short-range couplings of 200 meV, some 70% of the
total. Disordered zinc phthalocyanine reaches 195 meV within a stacked column, 66% of a total
that runs as high as 290 meV. These are not corrections. A calculation that drops them is not
approximating the coupling in a phthalocyanine stack; it is computing a different quantity.

The saving grace, and the reason the standard approach has survived, is that the effect
switches off sharply. Beyond about 7 Å of separation the short-range contribution falls into
the noise, with deviations typically under 1 meV. Chromophores that are merely nearby are
fine. Chromophores that touch are not.

A second result concerns the approximations themselves rather than the physics. Atomic
transition charges, the standard cheap stand-in for the full transition density, turn out to
carry systematic rather than random errors when used for the Coulombic coupling. And the
dipole–dipole approximation is worse than its reputation: at 6 Å it recovers only about 67%
even of the dipole contribution, and it does not converge onto the right answer as cleanly
with distance as the textbook argument implies. The approximation that is supposed to be
asymptotically safe is the one that misbehaves across the range.

## Where this sits

It extends a framework set out by Harcourt, Scholes and Ghiggino, who first separated
excitonic coupling into its Coulombic and short-range parts, from individual cases to a
systematic survey across both biology and materials.

Its most direct descendant is
[the multi-chromophore transport study](/work/multichromophore-dynamics/) published a few
months later by the same group, which puts atomic transition charges to work computing
couplings for fifty thousand structures. That is the sequence: this paper establishes where
the cheap methods can be trusted and where they cannot, and the next one spends that budget
on a question the expensive method could never have reached.

This is also the earliest work on this site, and the only quantum chemistry. Everything
afterwards is a machine-learned potential or something built on one. The connecting thread is
narrower than it looks: both are about when a cheap approximation to an expensive electronic
structure calculation can be trusted, and what breaks when it cannot.
