The Fenna–Matthews–Olson (FMO) complex is a small bacterial protein that holds 8 bacteriochlorophyll a (BChl a) molecules in each of its 3 subunits. The 8 pigments sit at odd distances and angles from one another, with no symmetry to the arrangement, and energy arriving from the antenna at one end of the protein has to cross them to reach the reaction centre at the other. In 2007 Engel and co-workers reported that the crossing looks wavelike. That one report made FMO the model system for exciton transport (an exciton being the excitation itself, the packet of energy that moves from pigment to pigment), and it started an argument that was still running in 2017: whether the protein's arrangement of its pigments is tuned for the job, or is simply an arrangement, one of many that would do as well.

My master's project at the University of Warwick, with Alessandro Troisi in the Department of Chemistry over the academic year 2015–16, set out to test that claim, and met a prior question first. {/* Q: 1 */} Any model of the transport needs a coupling for every pair of pigments, the number that sets how fast an excitation moves between them, and almost every model in the literature, the one in the second half of this page included, takes it from the Coulombic interaction between the two molecules' transition densities, often from a coarse version of it: a point charge on each atom, or a single dipole. Those are the cheap formulas. They leave out everything that happens where the two molecules' orbitals overlap, and nobody had measured, across a large set of real structures, how much that is. This page is therefore two papers from 2017, by which time I had moved to University College London (UCL), and they come in the order the question forced. {/* Q: 1 */} The first is the survey, with Troisi's group: 3 light-harvesting complexes and 2 molecular solids, every pair of chromophores, the full coupling set against the Coulombic one. The second is the test the project was for, run with the cheap method once its limits were known: 50,000 physically plausible rearrangements of FMO's 8 pigments, and the question of where nature's sits among them. The short version: for pigments not in contact the Coulombic coupling is the coupling, to about 10%; for pigments in van der Waals contact the discarded short-range part is up to 28% of the total in FMO and most of it in a stacked phthalocyanine column; and the natural FMO arrangement ranks 466th of 50,000, which the earlier way of asking the question could not have found, because it never built an alternative.

## Excitonic interactions

<Cite id="excitonic-interactions" />

{/* Q: 5 */}

The coupling between two chromophores has two parts, and the split is old. The Coulombic part is the electrostatic interaction between the two transition densities (the change in charge distribution each molecule undergoes when it is excited), and it dominates at long range. The short-range part, which Harcourt, Scholes and Ghiggino separated out in 1994 and 1995, is the penetration, overlap and Dexter exchange terms that exist only where the two molecules' orbitals overlap and that fall off exponentially with distance. Almost every exciton-dynamics model takes the Coulombic part alone, and for FMO in particular Jurinovich, Curutchet and Mennucci had shown in 2014, with a polarisable model, that most pairs are adequately described that way. The question the paper asks is the one that result leaves open: across a large set of real structures, how large is the part being discarded, and when can the cheap Coulombic approximations be trusted. The set was 3 light-harvesting complexes, FMO, the LH2 antenna complex of Rhodopseudomonas acidophila and the peridinin–chlorophyll protein (PCP) of Amphidinium carterae, and 2 organic semiconductors, crystalline octabutoxy phthalocyanine (OBPc) and a partially disordered solid of zinc phthalocyanine (ZnPc). The paper is Rocco P. Fornari's as first author, with Daniele Padula and Troisi, all of us in the Department of Chemistry at Warwick, and it carries no contribution statement. Second author. {/* Q: 2 */} {/* Q: 3 */} {/* Q: 6 */}

Every chromophore pair in the light-harvesting complexes was built from a crystal structure: for FMO the 8 BChl a of one subunit plus 1 from the neighbouring subunit, 36 dimers in all; for LH2 one third of the ring, from PDB 1NKZ; for PCP the pigments of PDB 1PPR. {/* Q: 4 */} {/* Q: 10 */} Only homodimers were computed, so the chlorophyll–carotenoid pairs are not in the set, and each molecule was given one rigid geometry, its phytyl tail replaced by a methyl group, superposed on the crystal positions with TINKER 7.1, which keeps crystallographic and force-field noise out of the couplings. The ZnPc solid was made rather than taken: 90 molecules annealed from the crystal in GROMACS 5.0.5 through 6 cycles of 700 K and 300 K, 204 ns in all, with 90 intra-column dimers (centre-of-mass distance under 6 Å, π–π stacking at 3.0–3.8 Å) and 10 inter-column dimers (6–15 Å) taken from the final snapshot. The 2 OBPc dimers, A face-to-face and B head-to-tail, with π–π stacking at 3.5–4.5 Å, came from Fornari's 2016 molecular dynamics with Aragó and Troisi as 250 snapshots 50 fs apart.

The excited states came from time-dependent density functional theory (TDDFT) at LC-ωPBE/6-31G\*\* (3-21G for OBPc) with ω = 0.3 a₀⁻¹, in NWChem 6.6, and for each molecule both the first and the second excited state, S₁ and S₂, with the 4 couplings between them, J₁₁, J₂₂, J₁₂ and J₂₁; peridinin was computed for S₁ only. S₂ is kept because the S₁–S₂ gap is 0.5 eV in BChl a, 0.6 in chlorophyll a and 0.8 in rhodopin glucoside, but only 0.02 eV in ZnPc and 0.03 in OBPc, and a pump-probe pulse of 10–50 fs carries a bandwidth of 0.08–0.4 eV. The Coulombic coupling J<sub>C</sub> was computed by the transition density cube method of Krueger, Scholes and Fleming: each transition density on a grid of 0.2 Å, about 10⁶ points per molecule, with Gaussian smearing and a relative permittivity of 1. The total coupling J<sub>TOT</sub> came from the diabatisation Aragó and Troisi had published in 2015, which fits the dimer's Mulliken atomic transition charges to the monomer's by an orthogonal Procrustes rotation (the rotation that best maps one set of points onto another). The short-range coupling is then J<sub>short</sub> = J<sub>TOT</sub> − J<sub>C</sub>, and the whole paper is about the size of that difference.

Before the comparison can mean anything, the sources of error have to be sized, and the paper sizes them. The functional sets the floor: J<sub>C</sub> differs by 6.4% and 12% in root-mean-square terms between LC-ωPBE and two other functionals, LC-PBE and CAM-B3LYP, for BChl a, and by 2.2% and 3.8% for ZnPc. The transition charges carry a bias: Mulliken atomic transition charges overestimate the transition dipole by about 3% for chlorophylls and phthalocyanines and by up to 20% for carotenoids, where the cube method is under 0.5% off (0.13% for rhodopin glucoside, 0.05% for peridinin). The point-dipole formula, the cheapest of all, was tested on a model face-to-face BChl a dimer pulled apart from 6 to 50 Å: the multipole terms it drops carry 67% of the Coulombic coupling at 6 Å and still 6% at 50 Å, and the formula comes within 4% of the cube value only at 100 Å, a separation at which there is not much coupling left to get right. Then there is the method mismatch. Even at 20–50 Å, where there is no orbital overlap to speak of, J<sub>TOT</sub> and J<sub>C</sub> differ by 3%, 13% and 11% for J₁₁, J₂₂ and the cross couplings, because one is perturbative and the other variational; so the paper treats a difference under 10–15% as noise and reads only the larger ones as short-range interaction.

The threshold that organises the result is a minimum intermolecular distance D of 7 Å, the closest atom-to-atom approach between the two molecules with hydrogens counted. Beyond it the couplings are small, under 10 meV, and J<sub>C</sub> is the coupling: the root-mean-square difference between J<sub>C</sub> and |J<sub>TOT</sub>| is under 1 meV, a relative error of about 12%, inside the spread between functionals. Inside 7 Å the same difference is an order of magnitude larger, and the paper's Table 1 lists the pairs that carry it. In FMO the largest short-range term belongs to pair 1-2, whose closest atoms are 2.26 Å apart: 4.29 meV of a total coupling of 15.2 meV, which is 28%. The other 3 FMO pairs in the table, 5-6, 4-7 and 3-7, at 2.18–2.61 Å, carry 19–25%.

In the molecular solids the short-range term stops being a correction. Between ZnPc columns the couplings are 1–30 meV and the short-range share averages 7.5%, by a linear fit; inside a column, where the molecules stack face-to-face at 3.0–3.8 Å, the largest short-range term is 195 meV, 66% of its total coupling, with totals reaching 290 meV against a strongest Coulombic term of about 100 meV. For scale, the reorganisation energy of ZnPc (the energy a molecule's geometry relaxes by after it is excited) is about 40 meV. Something unexpected also turned up in the signs. Wherever the Coulombic coupling in ZnPc exceeds 70 meV the short-range term has the same sign and reinforces it, and the face-to-face OBPc dimer A does the same. The head-to-tail dimer B does not – its signs are uncorrelated – and the paper is plain that the correlation depends on the particular wavefunctions and packing geometry and may not be predictable without computing the couplings. In OBPc the short-range share reaches 60% of the total, against a reorganisation energy of about 100 meV.

For anyone modelling an aggregate the paper states the consequence without hedging: its excitonic properties are set by its strongest couplings, which are between neighbours, so a quantitative description must include the short-range terms, and in the light-harvesting complexes the S₂ cross couplings as well. The limits are the ones the method chose. The medium's dielectric screening is left out (relative permittivity 1, where the literature scales couplings by 0.5–0.9); the geometries are rigid; only homodimers were computed. The result also cuts both ways, and the second half of this page rests on the other edge: for pigments not in contact, the typical discrepancy between the full coupling and the Coulombic one is about 10%, inside the spread between density functionals, so a transport model built on Coulombic couplings is standing on something, as long as it knows which pairs are touching.

## Multichromophore dynamics

<Cite id="multichromophore-dynamics" />

{/* Q: 5 */}

Is FMO's arrangement of its pigments tuned for transport, or would most physically possible arrangements do as well, and which features of a structure decide? Those are the two questions the paper poses, and the field had two ways of asking them, each with a hole. One sprinkles point dipoles at random into a volume and optimises the network, as Scholak, Wellens and Buchleitner did in 2011 and Mostarda and co-workers in 2013; it says little about real molecules, which are not points. The other starts from the Hamiltonian of a real complex and perturbs its matrix elements, as Caruso and co-workers did in 2009 and Baker and Habershon in 2015; the start is realistic, but a Hamiltonian perturbed element by element may describe no arrangement of molecules that could exist. Baghbanzadeh and Kassal, in 2016, had rotated and trimmed the chromophores of the purple-bacteria antennae LH1 and LH2 and found the natural arrangement 5.5 standard deviations better than the alternatives, and asked whether that is adaptation or a spandrel, a by-product of something else. The paper here is George C. Knee's, as first and corresponding author, in the Department of Physics at Warwick, with Luke D. Smith and Animesh Datta in the same department, Datta also corresponding, Troisi in Chemistry, by then also at the University of Liverpool, and me, by then at UCL; Knee is the collaborator my thesis names for the transport calculations that followed it. The paper carries no contribution statement. Second author. {/* Q: 2 */} {/* Q: 3 */} {/* Q: 6 */}

The method builds the alternatives, which is the whole difference. Start from the 8 BChl a of one FMO subunit, taken from the 1.3 Å crystal structure of the complex from Prosthecochloris aestuarii, as the paper's supplementary information gives it. {/* Q: 4 */} {/* Q: 10 */} Hold 2 of them fixed: BChl 1, the source, nearest the antenna, where the excitation starts, and BChl 3, the drain, nearest the reaction centre, where it is collected. Move the other 6 as rigid bodies, displacing and rotating them through 50,000 Monte Carlo moves, and reject any structure in which two pigments come within 2 Å of each other, atom to atom, or in which any centroid leaves a sphere of radius 22.3 Å about the midpoint of the source and the drain. {/* Q: 6 */} Those two constraints are what "physically plausible" means in the title: no clashes, and no drifting off. Every structure that passes is an ersatz FMO, in the paper's word.

Each ersatz structure gets an 8 × 8 excitonic Hamiltonian. The site energies, the energy of the excitation when it sits on each pigment, are held at the reference FMO's, from a QM/MM calculation in the literature, because no physically plausible way to perturb them was available, and the paper says so. The couplings come from the atomic transition charge method of Madjet, Abdurahman and Renger: a set of charges on each atom, fitted once to a TDDFT calculation at LC-ωPBE/6-31G\*\* (ω = 0.3 Å⁻¹) on the isolated chromophore, kept fixed as the molecule moves, with the Coulomb sum between two sets scaled by 0.8 for the protein's dielectric, after Adolphs, Müh, Madjet and Renger. Fixed charges are what make 50,000 structures affordable, since no excited state is recalculated. They are also, by construction, Coulombic couplings of the kind the first half of this page measured: adequate for pairs not in contact, and short by 19–28% for FMO's closest ones. In the reference Hamiltonian the largest coupling, between BChl 1 and BChl 2, is −154.04 cm⁻¹, which the paper puts at a rate of about 39 ps⁻¹, a coherent exchange every 25 fs. The transport itself is a Lindblad master equation, the standard equation for a quantum system that loses coherence to its surroundings: the excitation starts on BChl 1, a sink on BChl 3 drains it at 6.3 ps⁻¹, each site dephases at 2.1 ps⁻¹ and the excitation is lost to dissipation at 0.0005 ps⁻¹, rates taken from 77 K spectroscopy through Rebentrost, Mohseni and Aspuru-Guzik. The protein is in the model only as that dielectric factor and that dephasing. Transport efficiency η(t) is the population of the sink at time t, nothing integrated, and it was followed for 10 ps.

The 50,000 were then asked which features of a structure predict its efficiency, by Spearman rank correlation (a correlation between rankings rather than values, so it assumes no straight line); every correlation the paper reports is significant at p below 1 × 10⁻⁴⁵, and the ranks and spreads had settled once a sample passed about 1,000 structures. Two features carry the answer. The first is compactness, measured as the moment of inertia of the pigments about the source–drain vector: compact structures transport faster. The second, among the compact ones, is orientation, the sum of the projections of each pigment's Q<sub>y</sub> transition dipole (the direction along which it absorbs) onto that vector: structures whose dipoles line up along the route do best. Notably, both measures sharpen when they are restricted to a single pigment, the key chromophore, the one nearest the source–drain vector: the correlation strengthens by about 3.5 times for the moment of inertia and about 3.3 times for the orientation, and the key chromophore's orientation is at its most important in the first 100 fs or so, after which it becomes less dominant. The top 10 structures all have a pigment sitting directly between source and drain; the bottom 10 do not.

Where does nature's arrangement sit? The spread in efficiency across the ensemble is widest at about 4 ps, and at 4 ps the natural FMO structure has a normalised rank of 0.991: 466th of 50,000, with a transport efficiency of the order of 20 percentage points above the ensemble's. Most of the generated structures are less compact than nature's, and the paper notes that it is hard to pack the 8 BChl a tighter without clashes; the bias is acknowledged and tested with a control sample whose moment of inertia is held at the reference's, its variance under 1 Å². Two further controls say what the rank is made of. Hold the structure, and so the couplings, and randomise the site energies instead: the reference drops to a normalised rank of about 0.5, so given its site energies the structure is highly attuned, and given its structure the energies are not, which leaves room to improve FMO through its site energies alone. Vary the dephasing rate from half its value to 5.5 times it, a range that covers the 9.1 ps⁻¹ of room temperature, and the sink population changes by under 20%.

The control that matters most is the one that reproduces the earlier literature's method. Perturb the reference Hamiltonian element by element with uncorrelated noise, 3.5 cm⁻¹ on the couplings and 25.5–51 cm⁻¹ on the site energies, and FMO comes out generic, at a normalised rank of about 0.5, in an ensemble whose spread in efficiency is under 0.06, roughly a quarter of the geometric ensemble's. The reason is not subtle once it is said. A physical move of one pigment changes several couplings together, in a way fixed by the molecules' shapes and charges, and element-wise noise cannot make such a move; it explores Hamiltonians, most of which are no molecule's. So the earlier studies were not wrong about their Hamiltonians; they were answering a different question, about sensitivity rather than about alternatives, and the realistic starting point they insisted on was the right instinct. All 50,000 structures and their Hamiltonians are published, as [the paper's collection on figshare ↗](https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.c.3784874.v1), for anyone who wants to rank nature's arrangement against a different set.

The caveats are the paper's. The site energies never move; the transition charges never change; the protein is a number and a rate; the rates are 77 K values; and the choice of starting the excitation on BChl 1 is, in the paper's words, "the subject of some debate". My own master's thesis, dated 25 May 2016, holds an earlier version of the same test with a different verdict: under purely coherent dynamics and against 1,000 random analogues, the thesis's word for them, the natural FMO arrangement came out "only mediocre", with an efficiency by the thesis's measure of 0.67. Between the thesis and the paper came the dephasing, the two plausibility constraints and 50,000 structures in place of 1,000, and the verdict reversed. {/* Q: 8 */} That is the thing to take from the page, more than the rank itself. A result about whether nature's arrangement is special is only as good as the set of alternatives it was ranked against, and the alternatives have to be built, molecule by molecule, before the comparison means anything. Perturbing a matrix will tell you how sensitive a structure is. It will not tell you what else was possible.
