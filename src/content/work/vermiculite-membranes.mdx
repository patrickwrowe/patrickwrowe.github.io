This is an experimental paper from Manchester's National Graphene Institute with a
simulation component. Angelos Michaelides and I did the modelling; the contact angles, the
diffraction, the membrane fabrication and the separation performance are the Manchester
group's.

## The anomaly

Swapping the interlayer cation in a vermiculite laminate moves its water contact angle from
15° for lithium to 101° for tin — superhydrophilic to hydrophobic, in the same material.
Across most of that series, hydrophilicity tracks the hydration free energy of the cation,
which is the expected relationship.

Lithium does not fit. It is far more hydrophilic than its hydration energy predicts, and it
is the only laminate that swells further in liquid water and stays swollen — no change in
interlayer spacing after a week in kerosene, where a graphene oxide reference collapses from
13.5 to 8.5 Å in twelve hours.

## What the simulations showed

Ab initio molecular dynamics of thin water films on the two surfaces, in CP2K at PBE-D3 with
a 350 Ry cutoff. The surface model substitutes 25% of basal-plane silicon with aluminium,
giving the −2e per unit cell characteristic of natural vermiculite. Films of 40 heavy-water
molecules, 300 K, 35 ps for potassium and 40 ps for lithium.

The difference is in where the cations sit.

Potassium occupies a single strongly preferred site above the surface hydroxyls. That order
propagates into the water: the first contact layer is sharply peaked and orientationally
distorted, with a clear preference for one O–H bond pointing at the surface.

Lithium populates two competing sites — chelated inside the siloxane ring, or bound to an
oxygen next to an aluminium dopant. The resulting cation arrangement is disordered, which
gives more available adsorption sites, and the water responds: the orientational
distributions of the first and second contact layers are almost indistinguishable. The water
at a lithium vermiculite interface is close to bulk-like.

Water binds more strongly to the lithium surface in the contact layer, **−0.89 ± 0.02** against
**−0.69 ± 0.09 eV per water molecule**. The proposed mechanism is that potassium's few
adsorption sites force a highly ordered, high-energy interfacial layer, while lithium's many
sites let the interfacial water relax.

The same comparison in the second layer gives −0.7 ± 0.5 and −0.6 ± 0.5 eV per molecule. The
error bars there are larger than the difference, so the effect is not resolved beyond the
first layer.

## What it was used for

Building on the lithium result, the group coated a 30 nm lithium vermiculite layer onto a
polyamide microfiltration membrane. It holds roughly 7000 L m⁻² h⁻¹ at 1 bar after a week of
continuous oil contact, where the bare membrane fouls badly and a graphene oxide coating
survives about six hours before fouling worse than bare. Underwater oil adhesion falls below
the 1 µN detection limit, against 35 µN for bare polyamide.

## Where this sits

The simulation methodology — CP2K, PBE-D3, deuterated water films on a charged mineral
surface with a colour-noise thermostat — is standard for water at interfaces in the
Michaelides group, and the same broad approach as the interfacial water work in
[the aqueous potentials paper](/work/aqueous-potentials/) the following year, though at
direct ab initio cost rather than through a learned potential.

The explicit foil throughout is graphene oxide, where cation control of interlayer spacing was
already established. Vermiculite gives an 85° swing in contact angle where graphene oxide
gives about 15°, and its hydration does not collapse in oil.

No code or input files were published with the paper.
