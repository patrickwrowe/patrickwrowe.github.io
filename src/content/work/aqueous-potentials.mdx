Machine-learned potentials are usually built to be general, and generality is what makes them
take years. [GAP-20](/work/carbon-gap-20/) is one of the examples this paper cites of that
approach — my own work, cited as the thing being argued against.

The argument is that most scientific questions do not need a general model. They need one that
is right at a single thermodynamic state point, and that model can be built in days with
almost no human input.

## What we built

A committee neural network potential: several Behler–Parrinello networks trained from
independent random initialisations on overlapping subsets of the same data. The committee mean
is the prediction. The spread between members is a calibrated error estimate, and that error
estimate is what drives everything else.

The active learning loop starts from twenty randomly chosen frames of one short ab initio
trajectory. Train, then repeatedly add the twenty frames where the committee disagrees most
about the atomic forces. No new DFT is needed inside the loop — the reference trajectory
already has energies and forces for every frame. Convergence arrives at roughly 300 training
structures.

We applied it to six systems with no per-system hyperparameter tuning: fluoride and sulfate
ions in solution, water in carbon and boron nitride nanotubes, water confined between MoS₂
sheets, and water on rutile TiO₂(110). An automated protocol scores each model on radial
distribution functions, vibrational density of states and force RMSE.

## What it showed

Across all six systems: RDF scores 98–100%, VDOS 96–98%, forces 86–95%. For the
fluoride–water model the force RMSE is 56.8 meV Å⁻¹ on oxygen, 29.7 on hydrogen, 55.8 on
fluorine.

The resulting potentials are four to five orders of magnitude cheaper than the DFT they
replace, which is what buys the application.

On rutile TiO₂(110), 5 ns of 1728 atoms gives a density profile with two sharp contact layers,
essentially zero diffusion in the strongly adsorbed first layer, and an interface that
perturbs water diffusion more than a nanometre into the liquid. The committee error estimate
sits between 40 and 80 meV Å⁻¹ across the whole water region and does not drift over the
trajectory — which is the point of carrying a committee rather than a single network.

## My part in it

Third author, but the contribution statement records that Fabian Thiemann and I contributed
equally. I was one of three who designed the study, one of four who ran it, and one of six who
analysed the data.

Worth being precise about the boundary: the AML package that implements the active learning
is Schran and Marsalek's, credited to them under new analytic tools. My contribution is in
designing and running the model generation and its application across the six systems, not in
authoring that codebase.

## Where this sits

It builds directly on Schran, Brezina and Marsalek's committee neural network potentials from
the previous year, which established that committee disagreement tracks generalisation error
well enough to drive active learning. This paper is the application layer.

Read alongside [GAP-20](/work/carbon-gap-20/) it makes a chronology I find genuinely useful:
build the general-purpose potential, learn what it costs in effort and in accuracy, then argue
for the cheap disposable alternative — and cite your own earlier work as the foil. The two
papers do not contradict each other. They answer different questions about what a potential is
for.
