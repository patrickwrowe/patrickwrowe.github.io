Water next to a surface is not the water in the glass. Within a nanometre of a sheet of atoms it is layered, oriented and slowed, and most of what a membrane does, whether it lets water through, what it keeps out and whether oil can foul it, is decided in that nanometre. Two-dimensional materials make the question sharp because a sheet is all surface: a laminate of vermiculite or of molybdenum disulfide (MoS₂) is a stack of such sheets with water between them, and a carbon nanotube (CNT) is one sheet rolled round the water. The experiments on these materials are exact, and they turn up anomalies. The water contact angle of a vermiculite laminate (the angle a droplet makes with the surface, 0° for a surface the water spreads over completely) runs from 15° to 101° depending only on which cation sits between its layers, and lithium does not sit on the trend. A MoS₂ membrane shuts to water at low pH and opens at high pH, and remembers which it was last. Water slips through CNTs with a friction that falls as the tube narrows, and through boron nitride nanotubes (BNNTs), which have the same structure, with no slip the experiments could detect. Each of these came with a request for a mechanism, and in each case the request came to a simulation.

A simulation has a budget, and the budget is the subject of this page as much as the water is. In molecular dynamics (MD) the atoms move step by step under the forces between them, and in ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) those forces come from density functional theory (DFT, a quantum-mechanical method), which treats the water and the sheet on one footing; the interface is where the chemistry is, so that is what the question needs. The price is a few hundred atoms for a few tens of picoseconds. The 5 papers below, from 2020 to 2023, spend that budget 3 ways. The first and the last spend it directly, on questions sharp enough for 40 molecules of heavy water and 40 ps on vermiculite, or 103–108 molecules and 20 ps between MoS₂ sheets, to settle: why lithium breaks the vermiculite trend, and whether lithium changes how water moves inside a MoS₂ channel. One spends it on the sheet rather than the water, with a learned potential that lets 7,200 atoms of hexagonal boron nitride (hBN) ripple for about 0.5 ns. The two in the middle are the protocol and its payoff: a way of building a machine-learned interatomic potential (MLIP, a fitted model that gives the energy of a set of atoms and the forces on them from their positions, trained on DFT) for water at a wall from roughly 300 structures, and then the nanotube question answered with it over 40 ns and 16 tubes. The result I would keep from the whole page is from the tubes. Water's friction on boron nitride is about 4–5 times that on carbon at every curvature, and the two dependences separate by atom: the oxygen's free energy surface (FES, the map of what it costs the molecule to sit at each point over the wall) carries the dependence on radius; the hydrogen's carries the dependence on material. Oxygen makes water slip, and hydrogen makes it stick.

## Vermiculite membranes

<Cite id="vermiculite-membranes" />

Vermiculite is a layered silicate whose sheets carry a negative charge, balanced by cations that sit between them, and the cation can be swapped. Rahul R. Nair's group at the National Graphene Institute (NGI) in Manchester had exfoliated it and restacked it into laminates by ion exchange and vacuum filtration, 5 µm thick, and K. Huang, who did the experiments, measured what the swap does to wetting. {/* Q: 3 */} It does a great deal. In air the water contact angle is 15 ± 1° on lithium vermiculite (LiV), 56 ± 2° on potassium vermiculite (KV), 63 ± 3° on calcium (CaV), 75 ± 2° on lanthanum (LaV) and 101 ± 2° on tin (SnV): the same material, from strongly hydrophilic to hydrophobic, with a change of counter-ion. The contact angle of graphene oxide, for comparison, moves by about 15° over its cations. Wet, the LiV laminate goes to 0°, superhydrophilic, while every other laminate keeps its dry value; its interlayer spacing (the d-spacing, the repeat distance between sheets) opens from 13.6 Å at about 40% relative humidity to 15.2 Å, and it stays hydrated after a week in kerosene, where a wet graphene oxide laminate collapses from 13.5 to 8.5 Å in 12 h. Across the series the hydrophilicity decreases with the cation's hydration free energy, and lithium is the exception: far more hydrophilic than its hydration energy allows. The question that came to the simulations was why.

The modelling and the simulations were mine, with Angelos Michaelides at University College London (UCL). Second author. The surface model follows Sposito and co-workers: 25% of the silicon in the basal plane replaced by aluminium, which gives the sheet a charge of −2 e per unit cell, as in natural vermiculite, and enough cations on the surface to balance it. Spin-polarised DFT in CP2K at PBE-D3 (a form of DFT with a dispersion correction) with a 350 Ry plane-wave cut-off set the cation sites in vacuum, and the AIMD ran on a 2 × 1 supercell, 10.62 × 9.20 Å in the plane under 30 Å of vacuum, hydrated with 40 molecules of D₂O. Heavy water rather than light, because the heavier nucleus lessens nuclear quantum effects and allows a 0.5 fs step, and at this cost every femtosecond is spoken for. The film was held at 300 K with a coloured-noise thermostat, equilibrated for 5 ps, and run on 4 independent surfaces per ion, built from combinations of the vacuum-optimised cation sites, for 35 ps of analysed trajectory on KV and 40 ps on LiV; about 20 Å of vacuum remained above the film. What came out was the probability density of ion, oxygen and hydrogen against height above the surface, 2 angles for every water molecule, θ between the surface normal and the O–H bond and φ between the molecular plane and the basal plane (the face of the sheet), and binding energies from geometry optimisations of 10 snapshots per layer per ion, the energy of the slab with its water minus the slab and the waters apart.

The two cations do not sit on the surface the same way. Potassium has 1 strongly preferred site, above the surface hydroxyls, and takes it. Lithium has 2 competing sites, chelated in the siloxane ring (the ring of oxygens in the sheet's surface) or bound to an oxygen next to an aluminium dopant, so the lithium arrangement is disordered. That difference is the whole story, and the water tells it first. On KV the first contact layer is a sharp peak about 3 Å above the surface; on LiV it is broad and sits below about 4.5 Å.

Orientation says the same thing more sharply. On KV the first contact layer is orientationally distorted from the second, with one O–H bond of each molecule directed at the surface; on LiV the first and second layers have near-identical distributions of both angles, which is to say the contact layer on LiV is already bulk-like water. The paper puts this down to the number of adsorption sites and to the steric relief that chelated lithium gives: few sites on KV force an ordered, high-energy interface, many sites on LiV let it relax. The binding energies agree. Water in the contact layer binds at −0.89 ± 0.02 eV per molecule on LiV against −0.69 ± 0.09 eV on KV. In the second layer the numbers are −0.7 ± 0.5 and −0.6 ± 0.5 eV, and the error bars are larger than the difference, so I will claim nothing for the second layer, whatever the text says about the effect persisting.

The paper rules out one cause and leaves one gap, and both belong beside the mechanism. Surface charge density is ruled out as the origin of the wetting series, because the contact angle is insensitive to ionic strength while the zeta potential, the electrical potential of the surface as the liquid sees it, is not. The gap is that the simulations cover potassium and lithium only; calcium, lanthanum and tin sit on the measured trend, not in a trajectory. The application was Manchester's. A LiV coating about 30 nm thick on a polyamide membrane kept the membrane's water flux at about 7,000 L m⁻² h⁻¹ at 1 bar after a week in contact with kerosene, with oil droplets rolling off at under 3°: a fouling-resistant membrane, made by swapping one ion for another. A nanometre of ordered water, or the lack of it, is what the oil feels.

## Boron nitride rippling

<Cite id="hbn-rippling" />

Hexagonal boron nitride is graphene with the carbon atoms replaced, alternately, by boron and nitrogen: the same honeycomb lattice, electronically a different material. A free graphene sheet is never flat. It ripples, and the ripples have a theory, with a bending rigidity and a scaling exponent that decide how the amplitude grows with the size of the sheet. Fabian L. Thiemann, at UCL and Imperial College London, asked whether hBN ripples as graphene does, and what a sheet's bending rigidity does to the amplitude and the scaling of thermal and strain-induced ripples. Answering that on one footing needed an hBN potential at the accuracy of the graphene one, because comparing a classical hBN model with a machine-learned graphene one would compare the potentials and not the materials. The graphene one is the graphene GAP, the Gaussian approximation potential (GAP, a Gaussian-process fit of the energy to descriptors of each atom's surroundings) of my 2018 paper, which has its own section in [the carbon article →](/work/carbon/#graphene-potential); the hBN fit's cut-off and sparse-point count, and its treatment of the long-range interaction, come from GAP-20, the general carbon model of the same article. The paper is Thiemann's as first author, with Erich A. Müller at Imperial and Angelos Michaelides at UCL, and it carries no contribution statement, so I will say only what the author list says. Second author. {/* Q: 5 */}

The recipe is the carbon one with different atoms. A database of more than 22,000 configurations of monolayer, bilayer and bulk hBN and of nanotubes of arbitrary chirality, none above 200 atoms, from 0 to 3000 K and under uniaxial strain to 5%, all computed in VASP (a plane-wave DFT code) at PBE-D3 with a 1,100 eV cut-off. From it, about 1,600 structures chosen by farthest-point sampling (each new pick the configuration least like those already chosen) plus some chosen by hand for training, and 1,450 held out to test on. The fit is a two-body term plus a many-body term on the SOAP descriptor (smooth overlap of atomic positions), both with a 4.5 Å cut-off and with 5,000 sparse points (the reference environments the fit is built on) for the SOAP, fitted in QUIP to energies, forces and virials (the stresses on the cell). One step matters more than the hyperparameters. The interaction between two hBN layers does not tail off until about 10 Å, more than twice the SOAP cut-off, so a two-body model with a 10 Å cut-off was fitted to the interlayer binding curve and subtracted from the data before the main fit, the procedure introduced for GAP-20. Without it the model would have to learn, through a 4.5 Å window, a force it cannot see.

The benchmark is against the classical potentials people use for hBN: Tersoff, ExTeP (the extended Tersoff potential), ReaxFF, and Tersoff and ExTeP each paired with the interlayer potential (ILP) that supplies the attraction between layers they lack on their own. On the 1,450 held-out structures the hBN-GAP makes a root-mean-square error (RMSE) in the forces of 0.09 eV Å⁻¹. Tersoff makes 1.09, ExTeP 1.19, ReaxFF 6.17, and the ILP changes the first two to 1.11 and 1.22, because the ILP corrects what happens between layers and the error is within them. That is an order of magnitude, measured. Lattice parameters come out within 0.10% of DFT on average, where ReaxFF misses the out-of-plane parameter by 11.0%; Tersoff and ExTeP have a 2.0 Å cut-off, and the layers sit about 3.3 Å apart, so to those two potentials a bilayer is 2 monolayers that happen to be near each other. Formation energies land within 3 meV per atom, against more than 54 and more than 67 for Tersoff and ExTeP, which cannot tell one number of sheets from another, 45 for ReaxFF, and 4 and 26 for Tersoff+ILP and ExTeP+ILP; the 4 is the one classical number here that is competitive, and it is a potential with the interlayer term added. Phonons (the sheet's vibrations), checked against density functional perturbation theory (DFPT) in Quantum ESPRESSO, are within 5 meV at the Γ point (the long-wavelength limit) for the monolayer's optical branches and for the vibrational density of states of a (6,6) nanotube, where Tersoff and ExTeP overestimate the longitudinal and transverse optical modes by up to 40 meV and the acoustic modes by more than 20 meV.

Then the sheet was allowed to move. In LAMMPS (an MD code), a hexagonal box of 7,200 atoms, 15 × 15 nm when unstrained, with a 1 fs step, was held at 300 K under a Nosé–Hoover chain thermostat (a scheme that holds a simulation at a target temperature), first at constant pressure to set the lattice and then at constant volume, at relative strains from −2.0% to +2.0%, for about 0.5 ns per strain; the graphene side was run the same way under the graphene GAP. From the out-of-plane displacement field comes the normal–normal correlation function G(q) (how the sheet's tilt at one point correlates with its tilt one wavelength away), and from the way G(q) falls off with q come the two numbers the question was about. The first is the bending rigidity κ: 1.09 eV for hBN against 1.65 eV for graphene at 300 K, and 0.90 against 1.58 eV at 0 K from the flexural phonon branch, both higher than the classical force fields had given; the two-dimensional bulk moduli are 11.38 and 13.59 eV Å⁻². The second is the scaling exponent η (anomalous, because a thermally fluctuating membrane is stiffer at long wavelengths than its 0 K rigidity says): 0.82 for hBN and 0.87 for graphene, about 0.85 for both as the abstract rounds it, which is the value from earlier graphene simulations and from renormalisation-group theory. A previous hBN study with ExTeP had reported about 0.66; this result contradicts it, and the force errors above say which of the two to trust. hBN, the softer sheet, ripples with the larger height at every strain, and both materials cross from short-lived, incoherent fluctuations to coherent, soliton-like ripples (ripples that persist and keep their shape) between −0.25% and −0.5% compression, where an earlier suggestion for multilayer graphene had been −2.8%.

What it cost: several orders of magnitude less than DFT and about 3 orders more than the classical hBN force fields; the supplement's timing benchmark on 72 cores, from 8 atoms to 12,800, found DFT impossible above 2,048. What it cannot do, the paper says itself. There are no explicit charges, so the finite slope of the longitudinal optical branch near Γ (an effect of the long-range electric field in a polar sheet) is not reproduced; the finite cut-off leaves long-range electrostatics and dispersion untreated; defects and dopants are not in the training set; and “we do not claim perfect accuracy for each property”. Remarkably, the phase transition seen at 7,200 atoms is, in the paper's words, “exclusively based on an interpolation between the local environments in the training set”, none of which held more than 200 atoms. The paper's closing argument is also the hinge to the rest of this page: the vibrations of the sheet matter because the motion of whatever sits on it couples to them, so a potential that gets the phonons right is a potential you can put water on. The nanotube runs further down keep every wall atom flexible for that reason.

## Aqueous potentials

<Cite id="aqueous-potentials" />

A general-purpose MLIP takes years, because it has to be right everywhere its users might take it: the carbon one above did, and so did the silicon and phosphorus models the paper cites beside it. Most simulations do not go everywhere. They sit at one temperature, one density and one composition and ask one question, and a model that is right there and nowhere else would serve them, if it could be built quickly and checked without a person reading every radial distribution function (RDF, how likely a pair of atoms is to sit a given distance apart). That is the question of the 2021 paper with Christoph Schran, at the University of Cambridge and UCL, and Ondřej Marsalek, at Charles University in Prague, whose active-learning package the method runs on: whether a potential for an aqueous system can be built from a single short AIMD run with minimal human input and validated automatically, for the solid–liquid interfaces where AIMD cannot reach the needed time and length scales. Schran, Angelos Michaelides and I designed the study; Schran, Fabian Thiemann, Marsalek and I ran it, and Erich Müller joined us for the analysis. Which of the 6 models were mine the paper does not say, so I will not. Third author. {/* Q: 11 */}

The model is a committee neural network potential (C-NNP): several Behler–Parrinello neural network potentials, each a neural network reading the atom-centred symmetry functions that encode an atom's neighbourhood as numbers, with the committee's mean as the prediction and its spread as an estimate of the error. The spread is what makes the loop run on its own. The first committee is fitted to 20 random frames of the reference AIMD trajectory; it is then shown candidate frames from the same trajectory, the 20 on which its members disagree most about the forces are added, the committee is refitted, and so on until the disagreement stops falling. No new ab initio calculation is needed at any point, because the reference trajectory already carries the energies and forces of every frame. The symmetry functions are a systematic set, 10 radial and 4 angular, and the hyperparameters are the same for every system, which is the whole of the human input apart from choosing the system. The loop is the AML Python package, the fitting is n2p2, and the AIMD and the C-NNP dynamics are CP2K. The reference runs were typically 64–110 water molecules for 30–130 ps: fluoride in water at revPBE0-D3 for 50 ps, sulfate at BLYP-D3 for 30 ps, water in a (12,12) CNT and in a (12,12) BNNT, 288 wall atoms with 65 or 68 waters at 1.0 g cm⁻³, at PBE-D3 and 330 K for about 130 ps, water on a MoS₂ sheet of 168 atoms with 109 waters at optB88-vdW and 400 K for 30 ps, and 80 waters on 4 trilayers of rutile TiO₂(110) at optB88-vdW and 300 K for 30 ps. Then the check, which is also automatic: a C-NNP run of at least 0.5 ns, compared with the reference on the RDFs of every pair of species, the species-resolved vibrational density of states (the spectrum of the vibrations, by atom type) and the force error on held-out frames, each reduced to a percentage score.

All 6 systems went through the loop with no tuning for any of them, and every one converged at roughly 300 training structures. The scores are the figure below: RDFs at 98–100%, vibrational spectra at 96–98%, forces at 86–95%. The 3 systems of this page sit in the same range, MoS₂–water at 98.9, 96.4 and 86.8%, the CNT at 99.5, 96.1 and 92.7%, the BNNT at 99.7, 97.4 and 94.2%. For the fluoride model the panels show the force error by species: 56.8 meV Å⁻¹ on oxygen, 29.7 on hydrogen and 55.8 on the fluoride ion. What those models buy is speed, 4 to 5 orders of magnitude over the DFT setups they were trained on, at 1 state point each.

The payoff in the paper is on a surface that is not two-dimensional, rutile TiO₂(110), and it is off this page's theme, so 2 sentences and the figure above will have to carry it. {/* Q: 13 */} The TiO₂ model ran 1,728 atoms, 320 of them water molecules, for 5 ns, where the AIMD it was built from had 80 waters for 30 ps, and it resolved what 30 ps cannot: a density profile with 2 sharp contact layers, essentially zero diffusion in the first of them, in-plane diffusion rising toward the water–vacuum interface, a perturbation that reaches more than 1 nm into the liquid, and an adsorption free-energy map that converges only in the 5 ns run. Through all of it the committee's error estimate stayed between 40 and 80 meV Å⁻¹, 60 on average, with no drift over the 5 ns, which is the number that tells you the model never left the region it was trained in.

The limits are by design and the paper states them. A model built this way is specific to its state point; ±30 K away from the training temperature worked without problems, and pressure is left as future work. Each system has its own functional, chosen for it, because there is no perfect DFT functional for water, and the TiO₂ profile is more structured than earlier AIMD at PBE had shown, which the paper attributes to the functional and not to the method. The TiO₂ reference trajectory was Christopher Penschke's. The code, AML, is public under the GPL, and the data are on Zenodo. A general-purpose potential has been everywhere; these 6 have been to 1 place each, and they know it.

## Nanotube water flow

<Cite id="nanotube-water-flow" />

In 2016 Lydéric Bocquet's laboratory at ENS Paris measured water flowing through single nanotubes, one tube at a time, and found 2 things that did not fit together. In CNTs, water showed an enormous and curvature-dependent hydrodynamic slippage (slip: the fluid at the wall moves, where the textbook condition has it stationary, and the slip length is the distance behind the wall at which the extrapolated velocity would reach zero), with the smaller radii slipping more. In BNNTs, the same structure with different electrons, no slip could be detected, a slip length under 5 nm. {/* Q: 4 */} The paper cites the membrane measurements too, Majumder and co-workers in 2005, Holt and co-workers in 2006 and Keerthi and co-workers in 2021, but what Secchi, Marbach, Niguès, Stein, Siria and Bocquet had added was the tube-by-tube curvature dependence and the boron nitride control, and that is the pair of facts a mechanism has to explain. Why does water slip on carbon and stick on boron nitride, and why does the friction depend on the radius? Classical MD had answers spread over 3 orders of magnitude in the flow enhancement, which is a range rather than an answer, and AIMD, which could in principle settle it, had only ever managed flat sheets: Gabriele Tocci, Laurent Joly and Angelos Michaelides had found a 3–5 times difference in friction between graphene and hBN that way in 2014, and Poggioli and Limmer had suggested in 2021 that the hydrogen–nitrogen interaction carries the material dependence. Fabian Thiemann took the question to the committee potentials of the section above, with Christoph Schran, Erich Müller and Angelos Michaelides, the three of them corresponding; the paper carries no contribution statement. Third author. {/* Q: 5 */}

The paper built 2 C-NNPs with the 2021 workflow, carbon–water and boron nitride–water, trained to revPBE-D3, a functional chosen because it reproduces the structure and dynamics of liquid water and matches diffusion Monte Carlo and coupled-cluster interaction energies for water on graphene and for water inside a CNT. The training sets grew in 3 generations, set out in the supplement: (12,12) tubes at 0.6, 0.8 and 1.0 g cm⁻³, seeded from AIMD at 330 K; then bulk water and the flat sheets; then configurations from path-integral dynamics; 1,096 structures in the end for carbon–water and 885 for boron nitride–water. The production runs were in CP2K at 300 K, with separate thermostats (canonical sampling through velocity rescaling, CSVR) on the solid and the liquid, deuterium masses and a 1 fs step, and every wall atom free to move, so that the phonons of the tube could couple to the water, the point the hBN paper closed on. There were 16 armchair tubes, (m,m) with m = 12, 15, 18, 20, 25, 30, 35 and 40, 8 of each material, with radii from 0.82 to 2.77 nm and 130 to 2,136 water molecules at 1.0 g cm⁻³, plus a graphene sheet and an hBN sheet under about 35 Å of water; 966 to 8,328 atoms a system, at least 1 ns each, over 40 ns across the 18. Friction was measured at equilibrium, from the autocorrelation of the force the water exerts on the wall, the Green–Kubo relation of Bocquet and Barrat, with errors from 2 blocks of each trajectory. Then, for each tube, the FES of the contact layer was computed separately for oxygen and for hydrogen, with its corrugation ΔF, the spread of the surface, as the one number per atom per tube.

Water's friction coefficient λ on boron nitride is about 4–5 times that on carbon at every curvature, and in both materials it depends strongly on the radius. In the smallest CNT, radius about 0.8 nm, λ is about 1 × 10⁴ N s m⁻³; in the smallest BNNT it is about 6 × 10⁴. As the tubes widen the friction rises, and above a radius of about 2.5 nm it reaches the flat-sheet values, about 4.5 × 10⁴ on graphene and about 17 × 10⁴ on hBN. Put both series side by side and the smallest BNNT is about as slippery as flat graphene. For scale, the computational literature before this had carbon anywhere from about 1 to 10 × 10⁴ and boron nitride from about 4 to 30 × 10⁴, and the one experimental number on graphite, from Maali and co-workers in 2008, is about 12 × 10⁴.

Then the mechanism, and it is a mechanism by atom. The oxygen's FES becomes more corrugated as the tube widens, and it does so in the same way in both materials, so the dependence on radius is geometric. The hydrogen's surface hardly depends on radius, but it is far more corrugated on boron nitride than on carbon, so the dependence on material is chemical. The 2 corrugations, squared and added, line up with λ across all 16 tubes on 1 straight line. Where the atoms sit says what the chemistry is. Oxygen prefers the hollow site of the hexagon on both walls, and near boron on boron nitride; hydrogen sits near carbon on a CNT and near nitrogen on a BNNT, where a water molecule adopts a one-leg configuration, 1 hydrogen docked on a nitrogen, and moves by hopping from nitrogen to nitrogen. On graphene the hydrogen density is nearly homogeneous; there is nothing for it to dock on. In the paper's words, “oxygen-driven diffusion … in CNTs enables a large hydrodynamic slippage, water transport in BNNTs is hydrogen-dominated”. Oxygen makes it slip, hydrogen makes it stick, and the title of the paper is the result.

The disagreements with experiment are stated in the paper and I will state them here. The friction in the CNTs is an order of magnitude above the nanofluidic measurements, which were made on multi-wall tubes or on isolated tubes; the candidates the paper offers are quantum friction from electronic excitations in the wall, which Kavokine, Bocquet and Bocquet proposed in 2022, coupling between the layers of a multi-wall tube, and the rigidity of the wall, and it asks for measurements on single-wall tubes. The BNNTs slip substantially here and not at all in the experiment; surface charge from hydroxide on boron and defects in the wall are offered, and no water dissociated in any run. The classical spread of 3 orders of magnitude looks less damning once you know that a first-principles result is itself a factor of 10 from the measurement, and that is the fair thing to say about it. The supplement varies the density, the system size, the run length, the hydrogen mass, the functional, the nuclear quantum effects (thermostatted ring-polymer MD with 16 beads) and the wall's flexibility; the absolute values move and the trends do not.

## Molybdenum disulfide membranes

<Cite id="mos2-membranes" />

The last paper returns to Manchester and to the direct route. Rahul Nair's group had made membranes by restacking exfoliated sheets of MoS₂ in its 1T′ phase (a distorted, metastable form of the sheet, against the semiconducting 2H phase), and C. Hu, who did the experiments, found that they switch. {/* Q: 3 */} Soak a membrane in acid and it is shut to water; soak it in base and it is open, by a few orders of magnitude in vapour permeation and from under 0.01 to 1.1 L m⁻² h⁻¹ bar⁻¹ in liquid permeance; and it remembers. Going up from pH 1 the membrane stays shut until about pH 11; coming down from pH 12.1 it stays open until about pH 4. A 500 nm membrane switches in 2 min, a 2 µm one in 30 to 60 min, and 10 cycles of acid and base switched it 10 times with no degradation. Amritroop Achari, the corresponding author, ran the adsorption and vapour-permeation studies; Kostya S. Novoselov, at Manchester and the National University of Singapore, was in the analysis and the writing; P. D. Bentley and A. Pratt at the University of York did the photoelectron spectroscopy, and Z. Li and N. Zhang at the Dalian University of Technology the classical MD of water entering the gallery, which is not mine and which I will come back to. {/* Q: 3 */}

The experiments found the mechanism in the sulfur. X-ray diffraction puts the interlayer spacing at 11.4 Å for a pristine or base-treated 1T′ membrane and 6.4 Å for the 2H phase or an acid-treated membrane, the shut state. After acid the 1T′ fraction in the photoelectron spectrum falls from 56 to 46%, the S 2p line shifts by 0.5 eV to higher binding energy, and a new component appears at 161.8 eV, 16% of the fit, which is the sulfur–hydrogen bond. The pristine membranes carry 0.3 mmol per g of MoS₂ of lithium, left over from the exfoliation, and permeation is high above a Li/Mo ratio of 0.1. The picture, then, is reversible protonation of the surface sulfur: the proton neutralises the charge of the 1T′ sheet, the lithium that balanced it is expelled, the spacing collapses from 11.4 to 6.4 Å, and the membrane shuts; take the proton off in base, and the sequence runs backwards. The sulfur–hydrogen bond has a pKa of about 10 (the pH at which half of the bonds have given up their proton), which is why opening takes a pH of 11. {/* Q: 10 */}

What the experiments could not see is what the water does once it is inside. Angelos Michaelides, at Cambridge, and I designed the AIMD to look, I ran it, and we analysed it together. Third author. The question was narrow on purpose: whether the lithium content changes how water moves between the layers, “to understand the mechanism of water transport between the layers” as the paper puts it. The cells were about 500 atoms, 2 MoS₂ sheets held at a fixed interlayer distance of 1.0 nm with 103–108 water molecules and 1 to 16 lithium ions between them, annealed first with a classical model in LAMMPS (TIP4P/2005 water, a Lennard-Jones lithium) and then run in CP2K at optB88-vdW with a 550 Ry cut-off: 5 ps at 500 K, 5 ps at 400 K, then 20 ps of production at 400 K under a chain of 5 Nosé–Hoover thermostats. There were 4 systems, 2H, 1T′, and 1T′ at Li/Mo = 0.02 and at 0.3, with intermediate loadings run and found to vary little; from each came the oxygen and hydrogen densities across the gap, the in-plane oxygen density, the Mo–S and Mo–Mo bond-length distributions of the sheets and the mean squared displacement of the water oxygens.

Between the sheets the water forms a well-defined bilayer, 2 flat layers of molecules, and it does so whatever the sheet is and however much lithium is in the gap. The 2H and 1T′ substrates have very different Mo–S and Mo–Mo distributions, the 1T′ one broad and bimodal, and nearly identical water above them. The only differences between the 2 lithium loadings are the orientation of the water and the position of the ions: at Li/Mo = 0.3 the molecules rotate so that their hydrogens point at the MoS₂ surfaces, and the Li⁺ sit between the 2 water monolayers; at 0.02 the ions sit near the surface instead. The oxygens sit over sulfur sites, with a slightly raised probability between molybdenum and sulfur at the high loading, which the supplement reads as more hopping between sites. The mean squared displacement of the water oxygens, the measure of how far they wander in a given time, shows no substantial difference between Li/Mo = 0.02 and 0.3. Lithium does not change the dynamics of water inside the channel. Permeation, the paper concludes from this, is not controlled by the lithium concentration alone; and the classical MD of Li and Zhang then showed what lithium does control, which is entry: water goes into the gallery only when Li⁺ is already there.

The supplement is careful about what 20 ps can and cannot say: the “small observed difference in diffusion behaviour is insufficient to explain the dramatic changes” in permeation, and the effects of lithium and of protonation are “challenging to disentangle”. The data are available on request. So the direct route worked here for the same reason it worked on vermiculite in 2020: the question had been sharpened, by a group of experimentalists with a mechanism in hand, to one that a few hundred atoms and 20 ps can answer, and the answer was that something does not change. That is the budget to envy. The expensive part of a simulation is rarely the trajectory; it is knowing which 20 ps to run.
