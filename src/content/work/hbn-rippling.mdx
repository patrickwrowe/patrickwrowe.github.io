Hexagonal boron nitride is graphene's isostructural analogue: the same honeycomb lattice, with
boron and nitrogen alternating where graphene has only carbon. That similarity makes it the
natural control experiment for any claim about two-dimensional membranes, because a property
shared by both is structural and a property that differs is chemical.

Rippling is the obvious thing to compare. A free-standing 2D sheet cannot stay flat at finite
temperature, and how it ripples governs its mechanical and electronic behaviour. But comparing
the two materials fairly means describing both at the same level of theory, and at the time
that was not possible: the graphene side had
[a machine-learned potential](/work/graphene-potential/) and the hBN side did not. This paper
builds the missing half and then runs the comparison.

## What we built

A Gaussian approximation potential for hBN, fitted to tightly converged PBE+D3 reference data
from VASP at an 1100 eV plane-wave cutoff. The database spans more than 22,000 configurations: monolayers, bilayers, bulk and nanotubes
of arbitrary chirality, from 0 to 3000 K, under uniaxial strains up to 5%. Roughly 1600 of
those were picked out by farthest-point sampling as the actual training set, with 1450 held
back for validation.

The interesting technical problem is the same one GAP-20 had run into a few months earlier.
The SOAP descriptor has a finite cutoff, and in a layered material the interlayer interaction
has not decayed by the time you reach it; in hBN it runs out to about 10 Å. The fix, taken
directly from [GAP-20](/work/carbon-gap-20/), is to fit a two-body model to the interlayer
interaction curve out to 10 Å, subtract its energies and forces from the reference data, and
fit the main model to what remains. The SOAP cutoff, at 4.5 Å, and the 5000 sparse points come
from the same source.

## What the model does

Against 1450 held-out DFT configurations the force RMSE is **0.09 eV Å⁻¹**, against 1.09 for
Tersoff, 1.19 for ExTeP and 6.17 for ReaxFF. An order of magnitude better than the best
classical alternative. Lattice parameters across bulk, bilayer, monolayer and nanotubes come
out to 0.10% mean error, and formation energies to 3 meV per atom against 45–67 meV for the
classical potentials.

Some of that gap is structural rather than a matter of fitting quality. Bare Tersoff and ExTeP
carry a 2.0 Å cutoff, which is below the ≈3.3 Å interlayer spacing, so they cannot represent
the out-of-plane direction at all; ReaxFF can, and errs by 11% on it. Adding an explicit
interlayer potential to Tersoff repairs the formation energies but not the forces.

Phonon dispersions agree with density functional perturbation theory to better than 5 meV on
the optical branches at Γ, for both the monolayer and a (6,6) nanotube, where Tersoff and ExTeP
overestimate the optical energies by up to 40 meV and the acoustic modes by more than 20 meV.

One limitation is worth stating because it is intrinsic rather than incidental. The model
carries no explicit charges, so it cannot reproduce the finite slope of the longitudinal
optical branch near Γ. In a polar material that splitting is real physics arising from
long-range electrostatics, and a short-ranged descriptor cannot see it however well it is
fitted.

## What the comparison showed

The rippling simulations run 7200-atom sheets, about 15 × 15 nm, in LAMMPS for roughly 0.5 ns
per state point, at relative strains from −2.0% to +2.0%, with the graphene half driven by the
2018 graphene potential so that both materials are treated at equivalent accuracy.

Both materials give the same anomalous scaling exponent for the normal–normal correlation
function: η = 0.82 for hBN and 0.87 for graphene, against the value of about 0.85 expected from
the statistical mechanics of flexible membranes. That agreement matters in two directions. It
confirms that the scaling is a property of two-dimensional membranes in general rather than of
carbon in particular, and it contradicts an earlier report of η ≈ 0.66 for hBN obtained with a
classical potential.

What differs between the materials is stiffness, not scaling. Bending rigidities are 1.09 eV
for hBN against 1.65 eV for graphene at 300 K, and hBN accordingly ripples with a larger
amplitude at every strain studied, reaching about 9 Å of mean out-of-plane deviation under 2%
compression against roughly 1 Å under 2% tension. The scaling exponent is independent of the
bending rigidity: κ sets how far the sheet moves, not how the fluctuations scale.

Both materials also cross over from short-lived incoherent thermal fluctuations to spatially
coherent, soliton-like ripples, and they do so at the same place: between −0.25% and −0.5%
compression.

The model costs several orders of magnitude less than the DFT it replaces, and about three
orders of magnitude more than the classical force fields it outperforms. That is the same trade
as the carbon potentials, in the same proportions.

## Where this sits

This is the third model in a line, after [graphene](/work/graphene-potential/) in 2018 and
[carbon](/work/carbon-gap-20/) earlier in 2020, and the first built for a binary material
rather than an element.

It is the direct methodological predecessor of
[the defect-dependent corrugation work](/work/graphene-corrugation/) the following year, which
cites it, shares its first two authors, and deliberately matches its system size so the two are
comparable.

Worth being precise about one thing, because the neighbouring pages invite the wrong inference:
this potential is *not* what the [aqueous systems](/work/aqueous-potentials/) and
[nanotube water flow](/work/nanotube-water-flow/) papers run on. Those use committee neural
network potentials from a different methodological line, and neither cites this paper. The
boron nitride nanotubes appear in both stories, but the models simulating them are unrelated.
Two parallel arcs by overlapping people, not one.

The potential itself, with its training and validation sets, is deposited at Cambridge under
CC BY 4.0 and runs in LAMMPS through QUIP.
