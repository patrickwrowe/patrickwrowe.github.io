Simulating graphene at finite temperature meant choosing between two unsatisfactory options.
Density functional theory gives the right answer, but costs enough that a few hundred atoms
for a few picoseconds is a serious calculation on a national facility. Empirical bond-order
potentials run six orders of magnitude faster, and disagree with one another — and often with
experiment — about properties as basic as whether the lattice expands or contracts when it is
heated. Almost every interesting question about graphene at temperature falls in the gap
between the two.

This paper closes that gap for a single material. It was the first piece of work I did during
my PhD at UCL, and its central claim is narrow and testable: a machine-learned potential can
be made accurate enough to substitute for DFT in graphene simulations, and cheap enough that
the substitution is worth making.

## What we built

A Gaussian approximation potential, in which the total energy is decomposed into two-body,
three-body and SOAP many-body contributions rather than fitted as a single object. That
decomposition matters more than it might appear. Handing the smooth, short-range part of the
interaction to the low-order terms leaves the many-body descriptor to describe only what the
low-order terms cannot, which improved both the stability of the resulting dynamics and the
volume of reference data the fit required.

The training set is 1083 configurations of 200-atom sheets sampled between 300 and 4000 K,
computed in VASP with the dispersion-inclusive optB88-vdW functional and converged to
10⁻⁸ eV so that numerical noise in the reference forces stayed well below the accuracy we
were fitting to. We grew it iteratively rather than sampling it in one pass: seed the database
from classical molecular dynamics, fit a potential, run dynamics under that potential, add the
configurations it visits, refit. Each cycle populates the regions of configuration space the
model actually explores, which is not the same set the empirical potential used to seed it
would have chosen.

## What it showed

Against DFT forces on an independent set of configurations, the model reaches an RMSE of
**0.028 eV Å⁻¹** in-plane and **0.019 eV Å⁻¹** out-of-plane. The best empirical competitor,
AIREBO, manages 0.548 eV Å⁻¹; LCBOP 0.595, REBO 0.722, ReaxFF 1.226, and Tersoff 3.122 with
individual errors above 11 eV Å⁻¹. That is a factor of twenty over the best alternative and
a factor of a hundred over the worst.

Aggregate force error is the easiest number to quote and the least interesting, because a
potential is only useful if the errors that remain are uncorrelated with the observables you
care about. Three physical tests carry more weight.

**Phonons.** The 0 K dispersion agrees with the spectrum derived from inelastic X-ray
diffraction to sub-meV accuracy at almost every high-symmetry point. Mean absolute errors for
the alternatives run from 10 meV for REBO through 20 meV for AIREBO to 40 meV for Tersoff,
which reaches 110 meV on the E₂g bands at Γ. Every empirical potential we tested fails on the
dispersion of the high-energy longitudinal optical branch; the machine-learned model does not.
That branch governs Raman response and much of the thermal transport, so getting it wrong is
not a cosmetic failure.

**Thermal expansion.** Graphene contracts in-plane as it is heated to roughly 1750 K and
expands above it, with a total variation of only 0.1% across the range 60–2500 K. Reproducing
a non-monotonic effect that small is a demanding test, because the sign change depends on a
competition between out-of-plane flexural modes and ordinary anharmonic expansion. We
recovered both the sign and the magnitude against ab initio molecular dynamics. REBO predicts
positive expansion throughout, getting the sign wrong across the whole range. LCBOP predicts
strongly negative expansion with a spurious minimum near 1000 K. ReaxFF contracts sharply and
then fragments the sheet above 1500 K, far below any physical melting point.

**Raman softening.** The E₂g mode at Γ — the G band — softens as graphene is heated, and
variable-temperature Raman spectroscopy measures that softening directly. Comparing against
it required correcting the experimental data for the strain the graphene picks up from its
silicon nitride substrate, which is the sort of step that separates a genuine comparison with
experiment from a comparison with an idealised model of it. Once corrected, the
machine-learned potential tracks the measurements across 150–1400 K, as do REBO and AIREBO.
Tersoff greatly overestimates the softening. LCBOP disagrees qualitatively, predicting that
the band *hardens* over much of the range, despite having produced a respectable 0 K
dispersion curve — a useful reminder that agreement at zero temperature does not imply
agreement at finite temperature.

**Cost.** Normalised per core, per molecular dynamics step, on 200 atoms: Tersoff 1, the
machine-learned potential 340, DFTB 950, and ab initio molecular dynamics 2 × 10⁷. So the
model is roughly four orders of magnitude cheaper than the theory it stands in for, and two
to three orders more expensive than a bond-order potential. That is the trade the paper
proposes, stated plainly rather than buried.

## Limitations

The model is accurate because it is narrow. It is trained on graphene, and by construction
it cannot describe diamond or any other phase of carbon; asked to, it will fail confidently.
The reverse also holds, and we tested it — the general-purpose amorphous-carbon potential of
Deringer and Csányi, applied to graphene, gives force errors of 0.270 eV Å⁻¹, an order of
magnitude worse than the dedicated model, and a lattice parameter 1.2% too small.

Two potentials, the same framework, opposite failure modes: one flexible but imprecise, one
precise but narrow. The conclusions say so directly, and add that a general machine-learned
carbon potential looked feasible.

## Where this sits

The work builds on the GAP framework of Bartók, Csányi and co-workers, and takes the
amorphous-carbon potential of Deringer and Csányi as both a benchmark and a foil.

Closing the gap between the two became [GAP-20](/work/carbon-gap-20/) two years later. It
absorbed this training set wholesale and quantified what generalisation cost: 4 meV on
phonon frequencies at high-symmetry points, against 1 meV for this model, in exchange for the
rest of carbon chemical space.
