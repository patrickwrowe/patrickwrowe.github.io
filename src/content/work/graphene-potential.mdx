Simulating graphene at finite temperature meant choosing between two bad options. Density
functional theory gives the right answer and costs so much that a few hundred atoms for a
few picoseconds is a serious calculation. Empirical bond-order potentials run a million times
faster and disagree with each other — and often with experiment — about things as basic as
whether the lattice expands or contracts when you heat it.

This was my first machine-learning potential, and the work that became my PhD.

## What we built

A Gaussian approximation potential for graphene. The energy is decomposed into two-body,
three-body and SOAP many-body terms rather than fitted as a single object, which improved
both stability and how much data the fit needed.

The training set is 1083 configurations of 200-atom sheets between 300 and 4000 K, computed
in VASP with the dispersion-inclusive optB88-vdW functional and converged to 10⁻⁸ eV to keep
force noise out of the fit. It was grown iteratively: seed the database from classical
molecular dynamics, fit a potential, run dynamics with that potential, add what it visits,
refit.

## What it showed

Against DFT forces, the model reaches an RMSE of **0.028 eV Å⁻¹** in-plane. The best
empirical competitor, AIREBO, manages 0.548; Tersoff manages 3.122, with individual errors
above 11 eV Å⁻¹. That is roughly a factor of twenty over the best alternative and a factor of
a hundred over the worst.

Three results mattered more than the error statistics:

- **Phonons.** Sub-meV agreement with the dispersion derived from X-ray diffraction at almost
  every high-symmetry point. Mean absolute errors elsewhere: REBO 10 meV, AIREBO 20 meV,
  Tersoff 40 meV and up to 110 meV on the E₂g bands. Every empirical potential tested fails on
  the high-energy LO branch.
- **Thermal expansion.** Graphene contracts as it is heated to about 1750 K and then expands,
  a total variation of 0.1% across 60–2500 K. We reproduced that, sign and magnitude, against
  ab initio molecular dynamics. REBO gets the sign wrong throughout. ReaxFF contracts sharply
  and then tears the sheet apart above 1500 K, far below melting.
- **Cost.** About four orders of magnitude cheaper per step than the AIMD it stands in for,
  and two to three orders more expensive than a bond-order potential.

## The limitation, stated plainly

The model is accurate because it is narrow. It is trained on graphene and it cannot describe
diamond, or any other phase of carbon. We said so in the conclusions, alongside the
observation that a general machine-learned carbon potential looked feasible.

## Where this sits

It builds on the GAP framework of Bartók, Csányi and co-workers, and on the amorphous-carbon
potential of Deringer and Csányi — which we also used as a benchmark. That comparison is the
useful one: applied to graphene, the amorphous model gives force errors of 0.270 eV Å⁻¹ and a
lattice parameter 1.2% too small. Two potentials, the same framework, opposite failures — one
flexible but imprecise, one precise but narrow.

Closing that gap became [GAP-20](/work/carbon-gap-20/) two years later, which absorbed this
training set and reports exactly what generalisation cost: 4 meV on phonon frequencies against
1 meV for this model, in exchange for the rest of carbon chemical space.
