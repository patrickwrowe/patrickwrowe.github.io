[GAP-20](/work/carbon-gap-20/) describes carbon in every form it takes, and every one of
those forms is pure. Real carbon is not. Char, soot, activated carbon, battery anodes and the
biomass all of them are made from arrive with oxygen and hydrogen attached, and a potential
that cannot see a hydroxyl group cannot follow any of the chemistry that puts them there.

The draft I wrote in 2021 makes the point better than I would now:

> Despite the comparable excitement surrounding the potential uses and applications of the
> various pure carbon allotropes (graphene, graphite, carbon nanotubes and amorphous carbon),
> it is a fact which is often glossed over that these materials will not be free from
> oxidation and hydrogenation, even under the most ideal of conditions.

So the project was to add the two missing elements. The model is called CHO-GAP-22, and it
was never published.

## What was built

A Gaussian approximation potential over 3,167 configurations and 240,243 atoms: 183,656
carbon, 38,407 hydrogen, 18,180 oxygen. The reference level is CP2K at PBE with Grimme D3
dispersion, a DZVP-MOLOPT basis with GTH pseudopotentials and a 460 Ry cutoff.

Just under a third of the training set, 959 configurations, is carbon-only data carried over
from GAP-20 rather than computed fresh. That is deliberate and it is the reason the model has
a defensible pure-carbon limit: adding elements to a potential is an easy way to lose the
behaviour you already had, and keeping the old data in the fit is the cheapest insurance
against it.

The descriptor set is a two-body term at 4.5 Å plus six SOAP descriptors: a short and a long
length scale for each of the three elements as the central atom. Carbon and oxygen get 3.0 and
4.5 Å, hydrogen gets 2.5 and 3.5 Å, because hydrogen's chemical environment is decided much
closer in and spending a 4.5 Å descriptor on it mostly buys noise. About 12,000 sparse points
in total.

The one piece of real methodology is in how the fit is regularised. GAP fits need a per-configuration
estimate of how much to trust each force, and the standard practice at the time was to split the
training set into named categories by hand and assign each a tolerance. The draft is blunt about
why that does not scale:

> In previous models, this problem has been approached by manually separating the training
> data set into configuration types based on their known physical characteristics. While this
> approach has precipitated some success, it is not particularly extensible.

The replacement scales each atom's force tolerance with the magnitude of the force on it and
with the average over its configuration, so a quiet crystal and a violent liquid get different
treatment without anyone deciding in advance which is which. It is the part of the project I
would still defend.

The database was assembled over ten rounds of active learning: fit a model, drive random
structure search and molecular dynamics with it, find where it is uncertain, recompute those
configurations with CP2K, refit. The directory names record which chemistry kept failing.
Four separate rounds are labelled `RSS_OH`, targeted searches for hydroxyl configurations,
because O–H was the environment the model kept getting wrong and one round of patching never
fixed it.

## What it showed

The pure-carbon limit survives, which was the first thing to check: lattice parameters,
phonons, formation energies, defects and surfaces all reproduce the GAP-20 test suite.

Fifty small organic molecules from QM9, none of them seen in training, came out around
50 meV per atom, with outliers to 400. That is nowhere near the accuracy of models fitted to
QM9 itself, and the draft says so plainly: the aim was to be physically reasonable off-domain,
not competitive on someone else's benchmark. Liquid water reproduces AIMD radial distribution
functions at 300 K, with the same caveat attached in the draft, that this is explicitly not a
water potential and should not be used as one.

## Setting it on fire

The result I still like best is the one that costs the least to explain. Eight methane
molecules and sixteen oxygen molecules, which is the exact stoichiometry of complete
combustion, compressed into a 10 Å periodic box and held at 3000 K for 50 picoseconds.

The training set does contain the pieces. Among the reference states are twenty isolated O₂
configurations, twenty of CO, a handful of water and one CO₂, sitting alongside a couple of
hundred thousand atoms of condensed-phase carbon. What it does not contain anywhere is a
reaction. No trajectory in which methane becomes carbon dioxide, no transition state, no
half-broken C–H bond caught on the way to somewhere else. The model has been shown the
reactants and the products as separate static objects, and never the path between them.

Whether the path falls out of that anyway is a fair question and not an obvious yes.

It burns. Counting connected molecules in the final frame gives seven CO₂, one CO, twelve
H₂O, and the rest of the hydrogen distributed over OH radicals, free H atoms and one peroxide.
Carbon, hydrogen and oxygen all balance exactly, which is a check on my analysis rather than on
the physics, but a necessary one.

The honest reading is that this is a sanity check and not a validation. Nobody should quote a
rate constant from it. What it demonstrates is that a potential fitted to static condensed-phase
configurations, with no reactive data at all, does not fall over when handed a reaction, and
lands on the right products for roughly the right reasons. That is the minimum bar for the thing
the project actually wanted, which was to grow carbon from biomass rather than to burn methane.

## The result that was promised and never delivered

The abstract commits to something much larger:

> We demonstrate the capabilities of CHO-GAP-22 by simulating the formation of hard carbons
> directly from the widely available CHO containing biopolymers, lignin and cellulose.

That demonstration is not in the manuscript. The section where it should sit is bullet points
and an open TODO.

The work partly existed. Lignin pyrolysis was run and Jean-Marc Leyssale analysed 111
configurations from the trajectory: the oligomer fragments into pieces of five to forty
carbons, char grows, gas and oil come off, and the final char content agrees with both
experiment and previous ReaxFF simulations. On a Van Krevelen diagram against real type-III
organic matter it falls short, sitting at too early a stage of pyrolysis, releasing too little
oxygen and holding onto too much hydrogen. Char samples were sent to a lab in Nancy for
elemental analysis in March 2021 and came back at 92 to 94% carbon, so there was real material
to compare against.

None of that was ever written up.

## Why it stopped

Not for any of the reasons above. The model kept being improved, and in early 2022 an
iteration made it worse. From my own status notes at the time:

> V121. Trained with new structures and new sigma parameters. However, initial indications
> were not promising with phonon dispersion curves (etc.) being significantly poorer than with
> the previous "good" model (V106). Phonons for diamond and nanotubes could not be computed as
> the optimisation failed. Due to the number of changes made to fit this into 1Tb of memory,
> it's challenging to say what precisely was the cause.

That last sentence is the whole lesson. Several things changed at once because the fit had to
be squeezed into the memory available, and when the result got worse there was no way to
attribute it. The next model was a deliberate revert to V106 plus new structures, trained for
over a week on 1.5 Tb. By then the project had other demands on it and the manuscript, already
missing an introduction and a conclusion, never got them.

There is a smaller episode in the same directory that ended better. A note dated 4 May 2021
records that the Hartree to electronvolt conversion had been done with 13.605 rather than
27.211, so every energy in the collated database was out by a factor of two. The broken data
was kept rather than deleted, everything was recomputed, and the recomputation is a numbered
iteration of its own. That is the failure mode caught early. The V121 regression is the same
class of problem caught late, with too many variables moving to isolate it.

## Where this sits

It is the direct successor to [GAP-20](/work/carbon-gap-20/) and it inherits that project's
argument, that a potential is worth building when the useful unit of publication is the model
itself. The difference is that GAP-20 shipped and this did not, which makes it the clearest
example I have of the distance between a model that works and a piece of finished work.

I would draw two things from it now. The first is that the discipline that matters in a long
fit is version control over the training procedure, not over the code: changing the data, the
regularisation and the memory footprint in one step made a two-week regression permanently
unattributable. The second is about what to write down and when. The science here was
essentially done in August 2021. The parts that were missing were an introduction, a
conclusion, and one demonstration that had already half been run.

The same instinct shows up in [the work I did afterwards](/work/antibody-selection/), where the
deliverable was a decision someone would act on rather than a model, and the model existing was
never the point at which anything was finished.
