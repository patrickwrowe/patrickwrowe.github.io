The Fenna–Matthews–Olson complex is the fruit fly of quantum biology. It is a small
bacterial protein holding eight bacteriochlorophylls in a fixed arrangement, it shuttles
excitation energy from an antenna to a reaction centre with very little loss, and for a
decade it carried a large claim: that its structure is *tuned* for that job, perhaps even
tuned to exploit quantum coherence.

The claim is hard to test, because the obvious experiment is not available. You cannot run
evolution again and see what else it might have produced, and you cannot compare FMO against
the alternatives because the alternatives do not exist.

This paper makes them exist. Fifty thousand of them.

## What was done

The generative step is the whole argument, so it is worth being precise about it.

Starting from the crystal structure, the eight chromophores were moved and reoriented by
stochastic real-space transformations, under constraints: the volume enclosed is held fixed,
and no two atoms are allowed to approach closer than chemistry permits. The result is an
ensemble of *ersatz* complexes that are not FMO but could plausibly have been: arrangements a
protein could actually hold, rather than points in an abstract parameter space.

For each of the fifty thousand, the excitonic Hamiltonian was rebuilt from scratch: couplings
computed by the atomic transition charge method on DFT-optimised chromophore geometries at
LC-ωPBE/6-31G**. Transport was then propagated under a Lindblad master equation with
dephasing at 2.1 ps⁻¹ and a sink at 6.3 ps⁻¹, and efficiency read off as the population
delivered to the sink, integrated over 10 ps.

Two additional ensembles were generated as controls, and they earn their place. One varied
only chromophore *orientation*, holding positions fixed. The other abandoned geometry
altogether and perturbed the Hamiltonian matrix elements directly with uncorrelated noise,
which is, in effect, what the earlier literature had been doing.

## What it showed

Across the ensemble, transport efficiency at 10 ps runs from near zero to essentially
complete, a spread of some twenty percentage points between the worst and best ranked
structures. This is not a landscape where everything works.

The real FMO structure sits at the 99.1st percentile.

That number is the paper's headline and it does support the tuning claim, but the more useful
results are the two structural quantities that explain *why*, because those generalise beyond
one protein.

The first is compactness. High-performing structures are around 3.3 times more compact by
moment of inertia than poor ones, and efficiency correlates negatively with moment of inertia
throughout. This is close to obvious in hindsight: excitation has less far to go. It is still
worth having measured.

The second is not obvious. Of the eight chromophores, the one whose transition dipole
orientation matters is specifically the one lying closest to the line between source and
drain, and its influence *grows* over the transport window: the rank correlation climbs from
about 0.2 to 0.35 across the first 10 ps. The complex is not uniformly optimised. There is a
key chromophore on the path, and its alignment does disproportionate work.

## Why the controls matter more than the headline

The orientation-only ensemble gives a higher baseline efficiency but a weaker
structure–efficiency correlation. Reasonable: if you never let positions move, compactness
cannot vary, so it cannot explain anything.

The abstract ensemble is the informative failure. Perturbing Hamiltonian matrix elements
directly with uncorrelated noise produces a much narrower spread in efficiency, a standard
deviation around 0.06 against 0.2 for the geometric ensemble, and it does not reproduce the
structural correlations at all.

The reason is that the matrix elements of a real molecular Hamiltonian are not independent
quantities. Move one chromophore and several couplings change together, in a correlated way
determined by geometry. Noise applied element-by-element destroys precisely the correlations
that carry the structural signal. So a whole class of earlier studies, which perturbed
Hamiltonians rather than structures, was working in a regime where the effect being looked
for could not have appeared. That is a stronger and more transferable result than FMO's
percentile.

## Where this sits

It is the direct application of
[the short-range excitonic coupling work](/work/excitonic-interactions/) from the same year
and the same group, and the two should be read in that order. That paper benchmarked the fast
methods for computing couplings and mapped where they hold: the short-range terms atomic
transition charges omit dominate below about 7 Å and fall into the noise beyond it. FMO's
pigments are mostly further apart than that, so the ensemble here sits inside the validated
range. Fifty thousand Hamiltonians is only affordable with a cheap method, and only defensible
with one whose limits have been measured first.

Methodologically it sits between two earlier traditions and takes something from each: the
abstract "sprinkle" models, which capture generic principles but describe nothing real, and
the perturbative studies, which modify real structures but can wander into arrangements no
protein would hold. Constrained geometric perturbation is the compromise, and the controls
above are what demonstrate the compromise was necessary.

All fifty thousand structures and their Hamiltonians are deposited on figshare.
