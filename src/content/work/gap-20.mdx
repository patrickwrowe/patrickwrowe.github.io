Carbon is several materials wearing one element. Diamond is a rigid tetrahedral network,
graphite is stacked sheets held together by dispersion, a nanotube is one of those sheets
rolled up, and amorphous carbon is a disordered mixture of all of it. Historically, an
interatomic potential fitted to one of these was wrong about the others, and you picked your
potential by picking which part of carbon you cared about.

GAP-20 is one model that handles all of them, and this page is about the model as a thing you
can download and use. [The paper](/work/carbon-gap-20/) covers how it was built and how hard
it was tested.

## What you get

A Gaussian approximation potential — Gaussian process regression on the SOAP descriptor,
learning energies and forces directly from plane-wave DFT — fitted to 6,088 configurations
computed with the dispersion-inclusive optB88-vdW functional. The training set merges the
existing amorphous-carbon database with the graphene database from
[my earlier potential](/work/graphene-potential/), extended with point defects, surfaces,
nanotubes and other nanostructures, and recomputed end to end at one consistent level of
theory.

Two decisions do most of the work. An explicit r⁻⁶ term handles long-range dispersion, without
which the SOAP cutoff cannot see across the gap between graphite layers and the structure
simply falls apart. And the SOAP hyperparameters were scanned against held-out force error
rather than inherited from the literature.

On the crystalline allotropes it gets lattice parameters to 0.2% and formation energies to
0.5%. Defect and surface energies mostly land within 10%, with diamond defects the honest
weak spot at 25–35%.

## Why it was released this way

The repository holds the fitted potential, the complete training set as an extended XYZ file,
and a worked LAMMPS job you can run. Nothing needs refitting. That was the point: a potential
distributed as a methodology section is a potential nobody outside the group ever uses, and
the useful unit of publication for this kind of work is the artefact, not the description of
it.

The model and the training data are CC BY 4.0. The training set is arguably the more valuable
half of the release: it took far longer to assemble than the fit did, and it is the part
someone building a different carbon model would want.

## What it cost

Generality is not free and the paper quantifies the bill. Phonon frequencies at
high-symmetry points come out to 4 meV against the 1 meV of the graphene-only model: a factor
of four, traded for diamond, nanotubes, defects, surfaces, the amorphous phase and the liquid.
If graphene is genuinely all you need, the specialist model is still better. If it is not, this
is the one to reach for.

## Where this sits

It is the most-used thing I have built, at around 360 citations as of mid-2026. Most of what
followed on this site, at Manchester and later at AbCellera, is a variation on one question:
how much of an expensive calculation can a cheap model absorb before it stops being
trustworthy, and how do you find out where that edge is.

The year after, I co-authored [a paper](/work/aqueous-potentials/) arguing more or less the
opposite case — that most people are better served by a quick, disposable, system-specific
potential than by a years-long general-purpose one. It cites GAP-20 as the example of the
thing it is offering an alternative to. Both are right, for different questions.
