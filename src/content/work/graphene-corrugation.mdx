Free-standing graphene is never flat. A two-dimensional membrane at finite temperature ripples
and wrinkles, and that corrugation is not a nuisance to be averaged away. It controls the
electronic structure, the mechanical response and the chemical reactivity of the sheet. Real
graphene is also never perfect. So the question this paper asks is direct: how much does a
point defect change the shape of the sheet around it, and does the answer depend on which
defect it is?

It is a question that had been hard to answer for a specific reason. Answering it requires
thousands of atoms and hundreds of picoseconds, which rules out density functional theory, and
it requires getting the energetics of a *reconstructing* defect right, which is exactly where
classical bond-order potentials are least trustworthy. [GAP-20](/work/carbon-gap-20/) had
removed that obstacle the year before, and this is one of the first papers to spend it.

## What was done

Molecular dynamics in LAMMPS on free-standing periodic sheets of roughly 7000 carbon atoms, at
300 K and zero applied stress, driven by GAP-20. Two defects: the divacancy, where two adjacent
atoms are removed and the lattice reconstructs into one eight-membered and two five-membered
rings, and the Stone–Wales defect, where a single bond rotation turns four hexagons into two
pentagons and two heptagons. Concentrations run from an isolated defect at about 0.03% up to
3%, at which point defects sit roughly a nanometre apart and can feel one another.

Two choices in the setup matter more than they might appear.

Defects were placed randomly subject to a minimum 10 Å separation, with three independent
placements per defect type and concentration, and every simulation was started from a *flat*
sheet with the vacancy reconstruction left to happen on its own rather than being imposed by
the initial configuration. The honest way to run the experiment, and as it turns out the
discriminating one.

Corrugation is reported as an amplification factor: the standard deviation of the atomic
height distribution divided by the same quantity for pristine graphene at the same size. The
ratio is used deliberately, because absolute out-of-plane displacement in a flexible membrane
grows with the size of the system, so an absolute height would say more about the simulation
cell than about the defect.

## What it showed

Defects amplify corrugation substantially, and by an amount that depends on which defect it is.
At 3% concentration a divacancy-laden sheet is roughly five times more corrugated than pristine
graphene and a Stone–Wales sheet about three times, a factor of two between the two defect
types at equal concentration. The amplification rises steeply at low concentration and
plateaus above about 1.5%, and it is already well clear of unity by 0.2%. For scale, the
corrugation induced by 1% defects is comparable to that of pristine graphene held under 2%
compressive strain.

The mechanism comes out of fitting a local height function to the atoms around each defect and
reading off its inclination and Gaussian curvature at the centre.

Pristine graphene has an inclination of about 0.066 and, necessarily, zero Gaussian curvature:
an environment made only of hexagons is developable, so rippling in clean graphene is pure
local tilting with no intrinsic curvature anywhere.

A Stone–Wales defect has an inclination of about 0.270 in isolation, four to five times the
pristine value, and still zero Gaussian curvature. The tilt is intrinsic to the defect, not a
collective effect, and raising the concentration changes it by less than 20%. Stone–Wales
defects are strong, independent, flat-but-tilted seeds.

A divacancy behaves completely differently. Its inclination runs from 0.086 to 0.33 and its
Gaussian curvature from −0.001 to −0.012 Å⁻² across the concentration range: roughly fourfold
and tenfold increases. Negative Gaussian curvature means a saddle, a hyperbolic paraboloid or
a crisp, and the strong concentration dependence means divacancies are coupling to one another
rather than acting independently. Their perturbations superimpose and interfere.

So the two defects amplify corrugation by genuinely different routes: one imposes a fixed tilt,
the other imposes a curvature that grows as the defects begin to interact.

## Why the potential mattered

The paper ran the same simulations at 1% concentration with LCBOP and REBO II as a check. Both
agree qualitatively on the amplification factor, so at first glance the machine-learned
potential looks like an expensive way to reach the same conclusion.

It is not. Both classical potentials overestimate the divacancy reconstruction barrier badly
enough that, started from a flat sheet with unrelaxed vacancies, they never reconstruct and
never corrugate. They only reproduce the result if the reconstructed defect is handed to them
in the initial configuration: that is, if you already know the answer. A potential that gets
the right result only when told what it is cannot be used to discover anything, which is about
as clean a demonstration as you could want of the difference between reproducing a known result
and being able to find one.

## Where this sits

The simulations are driven by [GAP-20](/work/carbon-gap-20/), and the paper leans on the
specific thing that model was built for: a training set spanning pristine graphene through to
amorphous carbon covers the strongly distorted local environments around a reconstructing
divacancy, which a graphene-only potential such as
[the 2018 model](/work/graphene-potential/) would not. That earlier potential is cited too, for
the compressive-strain comparison. The paper describes itself as the first systematic study of
the interplay between defects and graphene morphology at close to quantum-mechanical accuracy,
and GAP-20 is what makes that claim available.

It is a direct methodological sibling of
[the hexagonal boron nitride rippling study](/work/hbn-rippling/) from the previous year: same
first two authors, same template of a machine-learned potential driving large-scale dynamics of
a two-dimensional membrane, and deliberately the same system size so the two are comparable.

The motivation is experimental: measurements showing graphene's elastic modulus *increasing*
with vacancy concentration, and defect-tuned thermal expansion, both of which point at a
morphological explanation that a purely mechanical picture does not supply.
