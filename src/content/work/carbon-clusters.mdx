Carbon clusters — chains, rings, fullerenes, nano-onions, amorphous nanoparticles — occupy a
structural space far too large for first-principles structure search to cover past a few tens
of atoms. The cost is not in generating candidate structures, which is cheap, but in relaxing
each one, which is a DFT calculation. Replace the relaxation with an interatomic potential and
the search extends by an order of magnitude in system size.

That substitution is only worth making if the potential relaxes a structure to the same place
DFT would. This paper tests whether any of them does, and it is the one paper on this list where
my own work is the thing being evaluated rather than the thing doing the evaluating.

## What was done

AIRSS — ab initio random structure search — coupled to geometry optimisation inside LAMMPS, so
that the expensive DFT relaxation step is replaced by a potential while every other search
parameter stays fixed. Seven carbon potentials were put through it: six classical, in Tersoff,
C-EDIP, LCBOP-I, ReaxFF, REBO-II and AIREBO, and one machine-learned, in
[GAP-20](/work/carbon-gap-20/).

The reference is a matched AIRSS+DFT dataset built with VASP at PBE, run under identical search
settings, spanning C₄ to C₂₀₀. Between about 3500 and 22,300 unique relaxed structures were
compared per method per symmetry class, on minimum-energy geometries, point-group symmetries,
cohesive energy distributions, sp/sp²/sp³ coordination fractions, ring statistics and radial
distribution functions. Each method was run twice over: once with symmetry operations enabled,
generating ordered candidates, and once in C₁ only, generating a deliberately disordered set.

Holding every search parameter fixed across all eight methods is what makes the comparison mean
anything. It is also what exposes a failure mode a straight energy benchmark would have missed,
which is the most interesting result in the paper.

Getting the relaxations to behave took more work than expected. AIRSS's default conjugate-gradient
protocol left a large fraction of structures sitting above 100 MPa of internal pressure — relaxed
by the optimiser's own criteria, but not actually at a minimum. Adding a FIRE damped-dynamics
stage followed by a Hessian-free truncated-Newton step brought them to essentially zero pressure.
Structures that are not properly relaxed produce energy distributions that look plausible and
compare badly, which would have contaminated every number in the benchmark.

## Gas-phase carbon, from chains to cages

The clusters here are isolated in vacuum — genuinely gas-phase carbon, with no periodic boundary
propping the structure up and every atom either bonded or dangling. That is a harsher test of a
potential than bulk, because most carbon potentials were fitted to condensed phases where
under-coordinated atoms are rare.

Against the DFT reference the structural progression with size is orderly. C₄ to C₇ are sp
chains. C₈ to C₁₉ are cyclo[n]carbon rings. C₂₀ is the smallest closed cage, and from there to
C₂₀₀ the clusters are predominantly sp², with the sp³ fraction below 20% until the particles
begin to densify above roughly C₁₂₀.

The classical potentials fail this progression in two distinct ways, and the failures are more
diagnostic than the energies. Tersoff and LCBOP-I hold a spurious 35% sp fraction all the way
from C₃₀ to C₂₀₀, building sp-rich surface shells that DFT does not produce — an artefact of how
they treat under-coordinated surface atoms. REBO-II and AIREBO fail in the opposite direction,
becoming trapped in over-dense sp³ configurations, unable to relax away from the compact
geometries the random search hands them. One family cannot stop making surfaces; the other
cannot escape the bulk.

GAP-20 tracks DFT on coordination, ring statistics and cohesive energy. Its energy distribution
sits under the DFT one in the −7.5 to −6.0 eV per atom band, while every classical potential
sits between −6.5 and −4.5, systematically under-binding clusters by of order 1 eV per atom. On
the C₆₀ icosahedral fullerene the cohesive energies run DFT −7.47, GAP-20 −7.57, then ReaxFF
−7.17, LCBOP-I −6.93, REBO-II −6.84, AIREBO −6.81, Tersoff −6.73 and EDIP −6.56 eV per atom.
GAP-20's C₆₀ radial distribution function sits almost on top of the DFT one, and EDIP is the
only potential that fails to resolve the twin bond-length peaks at 1.41 and 1.46 Å at all.

## Where GAP-20 is wrong

Worth setting out plainly, since it is my own model and I ran the searches that found these.

It over-binds C₆₀ by 0.10 eV per atom — the smallest error of the seven, but in the opposite
direction to everyone else's. It puts the chain-to-ring transition at C₁₀ where DFT puts it at
C₈. It gives a slightly larger sp³ fraction than DFT for disordered clusters above C₁₀₀. And for
the second- and third-lowest C₆₀ isomers no potential reproduced the DFT point-group symmetries,
GAP-20 included, though its energies were closest.

The most instructive result is a miss. In the broad C₄–C₂₀₀ search, GAP-20 — along with REBO-II
and AIREBO — failed to return Buckminsterfullerene among its minimum-energy structures. It only
appeared once a dedicated low-density search was run at C₆₀ specifically.

The right reading is that this is a property of the search, not of the potential. The same
protocol run with DFT did find it, so the sampling and the potential interact in a way that
neither reveals alone: random structures generated inside a sphere of fixed radius arrive at
a density from which some basins are simply unreachable, and which basins those are depends on
the potential's barriers. Benchmarking a potential inside a search tests the pair. It is an
easy thing to forget when a structure search is being used as an oracle.

## Large clusters, and the shift to amorphous

With the benchmark settled, GAP-20 alone was used to push the search from C₂₀₀ to C₇₂₀ — a size
regime out of DFT's reach, which is the entire point of the exercise.

Clusters stay predominantly sp² hollow cages up to about C₃₂₀. Above that the sp³ fraction
climbs and the structures become faceted sp² shells encapsulating sp³ cores, which is the same
transformation seen experimentally when nanodiamond converts to carbon onions, arrived at from
the opposite direction. Minimum-energy examples run C₂₄₀ at −7.70, C₅₄₀ at −7.46 and C₇₂₀ at
−7.17 eV per atom.

The more interesting finding is that non-canonical isomers compete. In the general C₂₄₀ search
the lowest structure found was a hollow *octahedral* pseudo-fullerene at −7.57 eV per atom,
beating the icosahedral pseudo-fullerenes the same search produced. A targeted low-density
search did eventually recover the canonical icosahedral C₂₄₀ as the true minimum at −7.81 eV
per atom — but with octahedral cages between −7.77 and −7.57, close enough behind that the
textbook picture of large fullerenes as necessarily icosahedral is less settled than it looks.

## Contribution

Third of five authors. I ran the AIRSS structure searches — both the DFT reference and the
potential-driven searches that the benchmark compares against it — and the large-cluster
extension from C₂₀₀ to C₇₂₀ was run under GAP-20 on the same machinery.

I should be straight about the conflict this creates: I am the author of GAP-20, and the searches
that concluded GAP-20 is the best of the seven were mine. The comparison was designed to be
protocol-identical precisely so that this matters as little as possible, every structure is in
the public database, and the failures above are reported because they are real, not as a
disclaimer. But a reader is entitled to know, and the published paper carries no author
contribution statement to tell them.

This is also the one paper here from outside the UCL and Cambridge orbit: my affiliations on it
are Happy Electron and the Engineering Laboratory at Cambridge, with the corresponding authors
at Warwick and Bordeaux.

## Where this sits

It sits downstream of [GAP-20](/work/carbon-gap-20/) and evaluates it on a class of structure it
was not trained for — isolated clusters, with curvature, surfaces and dangling bonds rather
than bulk or periodic environments. That is the interesting test, and the agreement holds up
despite the reference DFT here using a different functional from the one GAP-20 was fitted to.
An independent study across 1812 C₆₀ isomers, cited here, reached a similar conclusion.

The benchmarking tradition it extends is Carla de Tomas and Nigel Marks' work on transferability
in carbon potentials, moved from bulk amorphous carbon to isolated clusters. De Tomas is a
co-author, so this is a direct continuation of that line rather than a parallel one, and the
REBO-II densification failure reported here is explicitly traced back to it.

The full structure database is public on GitHub, though without a stated licence.
