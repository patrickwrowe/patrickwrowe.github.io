Carbon clusters — chains, rings, fullerenes, nano-onions, amorphous nanoparticles — occupy a
structural space far too large for first-principles structure search to cover past a few tens
of atoms. The cost is not in generating candidate structures, which is cheap, but in relaxing
each one, which is a DFT calculation. Replace the relaxation with an interatomic potential and
the search extends by an order of magnitude in system size.

That substitution is only worth making if the potential can be trusted to relax a structure to
the same place DFT would. This paper tests whether any of them can, and it is the one paper on
this list where my own work is the thing being evaluated rather than the thing doing the
evaluating.

## What was done

AIRSS — ab initio random structure search — coupled to geometry optimisation inside LAMMPS, so
that the expensive DFT relaxation is replaced by a potential while every other search parameter
stays fixed. Seven carbon potentials were put through it: six classical, in Tersoff, C-EDIP,
LCBOP-I, ReaxFF, REBO-II and AIREBO, and one machine-learned, in
[GAP-20](/work/carbon-gap-20/).

The reference is a matched AIRSS+DFT dataset built with VASP at PBE, run under identical search
settings, spanning C₄ to C₂₀₀. Between about 3500 and 22,300 unique relaxed structures were
compared per method per symmetry class, on minimum-energy geometries, point-group symmetries,
cohesive energy distributions, sp/sp²/sp³ coordination fractions, ring statistics and radial
distribution functions.

Holding the search settings fixed across all eight methods is what makes the comparison mean
something. It is also what exposes a failure mode that a straight energy benchmark would have
missed, discussed below.

## What it found

GAP-20 comes out ahead of the classical potentials on essentially every metric. The clearest
statement is the cohesive energy distribution: DFT and GAP-20 both populate the −7.5 to
−6.0 eV per atom band, while every classical potential sits between −6.5 and −4.5, systematically
under-binding clusters by of order 1 eV per atom. On the C₆₀ icosahedral fullerene the cohesive
energies run DFT −7.47, GAP-20 −7.57, then ReaxFF −7.17, LCBOP-I −6.93, REBO-II −6.84, AIREBO
−6.81, Tersoff −6.73 and EDIP −6.56 eV per atom. The C₆₀ radial distribution function from
GAP-20 sits almost on top of the DFT one, and EDIP is the only potential that fails to resolve
the twin bond-length peaks at 1.41 and 1.46 Å at all.

The coordination benchmark separates the classical potentials by failure mode rather than by
accuracy. Tersoff and LCBOP-I hold a spurious 35% sp fraction from C₃₀ to C₂₀₀, building
sp-rich surface shells that DFT does not produce. REBO-II and AIREBO fail the other way, getting
trapped in over-dense sp³ configurations and unable to relax away from the compact geometries
the search hands them.

Where GAP-20 is wrong is worth setting out plainly, since it is my own model.

It over-binds the C₆₀ fullerene by 0.10 eV per atom — the smallest error of the seven, but an
error in the opposite direction to everyone else's. It puts the chain-to-ring transition at C₁₀
where DFT puts it at C₈. It gives a slightly larger sp³ fraction than DFT for disordered
clusters above C₁₀₀. And for the second- and third-lowest C₆₀ isomers no potential reproduced
the DFT point-group symmetries, GAP-20 included, though its energies were the closest.

The most instructive result is a miss. In the broad C₄–C₂₀₀ search, GAP-20 — along with REBO-II
and AIREBO — failed to return Buckminsterfullerene among its minimum-energy structures. It only
appeared once a dedicated low-density C₆₀ search was run. The authors attribute this to a
density bias in the search protocol rather than to the potential, which is the right reading:
the same protocol run with DFT found it, so the sampling and the potential interact in a way
neither reveals alone. It is a useful reminder that benchmarking a potential inside a search
tests the pair, not the potential.

## What it was used for

With the benchmark settled, GAP-20 alone was used to push the search from C₂₀₀ to C₇₂₀ — a size
regime out of DFT's reach, which is the whole point of the exercise.

Clusters stay predominantly sp² hollow cages up to about C₃₂₀, after which the sp³ fraction
climbs and the structures become faceted sp² shells encapsulating sp³ cores, echoing the
nanodiamond-to-carbon-onion transformations seen experimentally. Minimum-energy examples run
C₂₄₀ at −7.70, C₅₄₀ at −7.46 and C₇₂₀ at −7.17 eV per atom.

The more interesting finding is that non-canonical isomers compete. In the general C₂₄₀ search
the lowest structure found was a hollow *octahedral* pseudo-fullerene at −7.57 eV per atom,
beating the icosahedral pseudo-fullerenes the same search produced. A targeted low-density
search did eventually recover the canonical icosahedral C₂₄₀ as the true minimum at −7.81 eV per
atom — but with octahedral cages between −7.77 and −7.57, close enough behind that the textbook
picture of large fullerenes as icosahedral is less settled than it looks.

## Where this sits

It sits downstream of [GAP-20](/work/carbon-gap-20/) and evaluates it on a class of structure
it was not trained for — isolated clusters, with curvature and dangling bonds rather than bulk
or periodic environments. That is the interesting test, and the model's agreement holds up
despite the reference DFT here using a different functional from the one GAP-20 was fitted to.
An independent study of GAP-20 across 1812 C₆₀ isomers, cited here, reached a similar
conclusion.

The benchmarking tradition it extends is Carla de Tomas and Nigel Marks' work on transferability
in carbon potentials, moved from bulk amorphous carbon to isolated clusters — de Tomas is a
co-author, so this is a direct continuation of that line rather than a parallel one. The
REBO-II densification failure reported here is explicitly traced back to it.

The full structure database is public on GitHub, though without a stated licence.
