A T-cell engager (TCE) is an antibody with a job on each end. One arm grips an antigen on a tumour cell, the other grips CD3 on a T cell, and holding the two together makes the T cell kill something it would otherwise walk past. The discovery effort goes into the tumour arm, because that is the part that changes between programmes, and the CD3 arm is treated as a fixed component: find one with reasonable affinity, reuse it everywhere. Most TCEs in clinical development descend from a small number of CD3-binding antibodies, SP34-2 among them; it was described in 1985. The molecule that comes out of this has to maximise tumour-cell killing while fine-tuning cytokine release, two readings taken side by side in every functional assay on this page, and the field's own account of where it stands is that "the full extent to which CD3-binding parameters impact TCE function is not well understood".

This page is four conference posters from 2023 and 2024, out of three years of work at AbCellera in Vancouver {/* Q: 4 */} {/* Q: 6 */} across more than thirty clinical programmes{/* Q: 5 */}; they are the published part. The first asks whether a CD3 arm can be chosen on evidence rather than on habit, and answers by measuring what every binder in the portfolio does instead of what it binds: 19 functional clusters, and selection across clusters rather than within them. The next two follow one programme that spent those clusters on a target that is not on the cell surface at all, a MAGE-A4 peptide displayed on MHC class I, and end with a bispecific that bound none of more than 180 non-MAGE peptides it was shown. The fourth is the platform in the round, and the levers it has beyond the two arms. If you have ever had to pick a handful of molecules out of a panel far too large to characterise properly, the problem will be familiar whatever the molecules were made of.

## CD3 binder selection

<Cite id="cd3-binder-selection" />

There are two cheap ways to choose a CD3 arm. Reuse the one that worked last time, or rank the panel by affinity and take the top of it. Both assume that a binder has a profile of its own, something it carries from programme to programme, and the first thing this work did was establish that it does not: the same CD3 binder gives materially different function depending on which tumour arm it is paired with, which the group had shown for an earlier panel; the poster's own caption says flatly that functional profiles for a new tumour target "cannot be accurately predicted" from a binder's behaviour with another. That is the problem the rest of the procedure exists to solve. If a binder has no context-free profile, it has to be measured in context, in numbers large enough to choose from.

The panel was built, therefore, to isolate the variable. Every CD3-binding antibody in the portfolio was paired with a single tumour-binding arm, so that the CD3 arm was the only thing changing, and the resulting bispecifics were expressed and run through high-throughput functional assays: unactivated human T cells with tumour cells at an effector-to-target ratio of 10:1 for 48 hours, reading tumour-cell killing and the release of IFNγ, IL-2 and TNFα. Surface plasmon resonance (SPR, which measures binding and unbinding at a surface in real time) gave the kinetics against the human CD3 εδ subunit pair for comparison. The functional readouts were normalised to the interval 0 to 1, each antibody was placed high, medium or low on each feature by its distance from the feature mean, and the antibodies were clustered on those profiles.

Kinetics turned out to describe some of it and not enough of it. Off rate is the primary driver of affinity and a significant contributor to both cytokine release and tumour-cell killing, but it "was not sufficient to fully describe these properties, particularly at faster off rates", and potency was not tightly correlated with off rate or with clonal family. The poster makes the point with three example antibodies. Antibodies 1 and 2 have similar kinetics, 320 nM and 600 nM affinity at off rates of 4 × 10⁻² and 2 × 10⁻² s⁻¹, and land in different functional places: 40% against 75% maximum killing, and 772 against 5.7 × 10⁴ pg mL⁻¹ of IFNγ. Antibodies 2 and 3 have much the same functional profile, 75% and 73% killing with IL-2 at 81 and 102 pg mL⁻¹, from kinetics an order of magnitude apart, 600 nM at 2 × 10⁻² s⁻¹ against 38 nM at 3 × 10⁻³ s⁻¹. All three come from different clonal families; neither sequence ancestry nor the biophysics predicts the behaviour. Ranking a panel of CD3 binders by affinity and hoping that function follows is, on this evidence, a lottery with a plausible-looking ticket.

Cluster the portfolio on what its members do and it resolves into 19 clusters. That number is the useful object here, more than any individual antibody is: the clusters, not the antibodies, became the unit of selection, so that a programme takes arms from distinct clusters, pairs them with diverse tumour arms, profiles hundreds of bispecifics, carries dozens into a lead panel, takes about 1 to 3 forward as development candidates for the studies that precede a trial, and may go back for more from whichever cluster performed best. Not every cluster size can be read off the panel, however: only clusters holding more than 10 antibodies are labelled.

The procedure ran end to end on two programmes, and their funnels are the clearest statement of what it costs. For PSMA: more than 300 CD3 binders, grouped into the 19 clusters, gave 10 CD3 parentals spanning 1 nM to 12 µM in affinity; separately, more than 400 PSMA binders gave 92 expressed and characterised and 9 parentals, drawn from 5 epitope bins and spanning 46 pM to 31 nM; the two sets together made more than 180 bispecifics, from which 9 leads were picked out, assayed on LNCaP cells. For MAGE-A4, an intracellular antigen read as a peptide: the same 300-plus CD3 binders and the same 19 clusters, this time 19 CD3 parentals, one from every cluster, spanning 1 nM to 3 µM; 200 MAGE-A4 binders gave 45 expressed and characterised and 6 parentals; more than 200 bispecifics in the 1x1 format, 12 leads, assayed on A375 cells and on A375 with MAGE-A4 knocked out. Over 380 bispecifics across the two programmes, then, built and profiled to arrive at lead panels of 9 and 12.

The two programmes are not a matched comparison of the method, and it is worth saying so before anyone treats them as one: cross-reactivity with cynomolgus macaque (cyno) was a filter on the PSMA selection and was not required for MAGE-A4, which let that programme select from a broader range of functional profiles. What the lead panels did show is that the PSMA panel's profiles span the range of the clinical molecules it was set beside, TNB-585 and AMG-160, and that the MAGE-A4 panel, set beside Molecule R, held the functional profiles the programme wanted together with peptide–MHC specificity. ## MAGE-A4 engagers

<Cite id="tce-pmhc" /> {/* Q: 10 */}

A T-cell engager can only attack what it can see, and what it can see is the cell surface. Fewer than 15% of cellular proteins go there. The more than 85% that do not are intracellular and, by the usual logic, unreachable. That logic forgets peptide–MHC: cells chop up their own intracellular proteins and display the fragments on MHC class I, so an intracellular antigen is readable from outside as a short peptide in a groove. Target that, and the pool of things a TCE can be aimed at stops being a minority of the proteome.

The catch is specificity: the thing being recognised is 10 amino acids lying in a groove on a protein that is otherwise identical on every cell in the body. Antibodies are good at this in one respect, binding peptide–MHC (pMHC) natively at nanomolar to picomolar affinities where T-cell receptors have to be engineered up to get there, and bad at it in another: they often bind with a bias towards the ends of the peptide, which can reduce specificity and, as poster 2373 puts it, "necessitates extensive specificity screening to avoid off-target toxicities". The programme on this page is largely that screening.

The discovery end was proprietary immunisation and single-cell screening: 1.5 million single cells interrogated, 200 unique sequences binding the MAGE-A4 pMHC, 45 expressed and characterised, and 6 taken forward, from 4 clonal families and with a spread of CDR3 lengths and V-gene usage, so that the 6 were not 6 versions of one antibody. They are fully human sequences and they were not affinity-matured; their affinities by SPR run from 7 nM to 15 µM, which is a wide window to select inside and a reminder that affinity was not what was being selected for. Specificity came first from a peptide-pulsed T2 assay against the MAGE-A4 pMHC and 19 closely related ones, and then from an X-scan: every amino acid at every one of the 10 positions, each variant pulsed onto T2 cells and read by flow cytometry, with anything under 1.5-fold over non-pulsed T2 cells counted as no complex formed and dropped from the analysis. {/* Q: 9 */} Developability was 6 assays, CE-SDS, aSEC, aHIC, nDSF, BVP-ELISA and AC-SINS, run early rather than at the end, because a specific binder that cannot be manufactured is not a candidate.

Then the two halves were put together: the 6 tumour arms were paired with 19 CD3 arms from the clustered platform of the section above, using AbCellera's OrthoMab™ multispecific engineering, which gave more than 200 bispecifics in the 1x1 format. The dependency between the two sections is sourced rather than asserted: the AACR poster cites the CD3-selection poster in its own reference list as the source of those arms. Function was killing of MAGE-A4-expressing A375 cells against the MAGE-A4 knockout line at 10:1 for 48 hours with the three cytokines beside it, which widened a year later to several lines, MAGE-A4-positive A375 and NCI-H1703, their knockouts, and MAGE-A4-negative CAMA-1, at 10:1 or 20:1 for 72 hours. Of the 200-plus, 12 went to deep characterisation and 1 to preclinical assessment.

What the structures say is that the antibodies read the peptide rather than its housing. Cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) of 4 Fab–pMHC complexes at 2.7 to 3.3 Å shows peptide-centric binding, with the arms approaching at diverse angles and orientations rather than converging on one pose, and the X-scan puts the weight of recognition on arginine at position 6, whose side chain is fully solvent-exposed. Peptide-centric binding is the property worth having and also the one that makes the screening necessary, since a binder reading one exposed side chain will read that side chain wherever else it is presented.

The result the programme turns on is stated on that poster with its exception inside it: the lead "specifically binds tumor-associated peptides from MAGE-A4- and MAGE-A8-pMHC but not to any other peptides tested". More than 180 non-MAGE peptides, nothing. Within the MAGE family, two antigens rather than one, MAGE-A4 and the closely related MAGE-A8, and MAGE-A8 was not a surprise found late: the SPR panel on the earlier poster already ran MAGE-A8 pMHC beside MAGE-A4 pMHC, so it was being watched from the beginning. A binder restricted to two related tumour antigens is the finding, and it is a different kind of statement when the panel it was shown runs past 180.

The posters are candid about the rest of it. Molecule D killed MAGE-A4 knockout cells at higher concentrations and so did not meet the desired profile. Substitutions that stopped the peptide loading onto MHC class I at all were excluded from the X-scan, so the scan speaks only for peptides that still load. Neither poster reports a dissociation constant for the lead, a deposited structure, or where the 180-plus peptide panel came from, and the earlier poster's own next steps list further specificity work, more cell lines, T-cell activation, proliferation and exhaustion, developability and in vivo efficacy: a programme caught in the middle, twice, which is what a conference poster is for. Author on both.

## Selection platform

The two programmes above are the published end of something larger: what three years of the work actually consisted of. It had three layers, and the least glamorous one mattered most. Data: high-throughput antibody discovery runs on lab automation, and lab automation produces data that is voluminous, heterogeneous and rarely assembled with modelling in mind, so a large fraction of the effort went into the pipelines and the data architecture underneath everything else, because a scoring function is worth nothing if the assay results it is trained on cannot be joined reliably to the sequences that produced them. Simulation: structure-based work on the molecules themselves, the interface a binder makes with its target, and the properties that decide whether a good binder can be manufactured, formulated and dosed at all. Scoring: models over both, aimed at ranking and triage rather than at prediction for its own sake, where the output that matters is a shortlist a team will actually run. {/* Q: 4 */}

Poster 1291 is the one public document that describes the platform beyond those two programmes, and it starts from a gap in the field rather than from a molecule. Solid tumours are more than 90% of all cancers, and 7 of the 9 T-cell engagers approved when the poster was presented in 2024 treat haematological ones. Widening the therapeutic window of a solid-tumour TCE, more killing without more cytokine release, is the problem, and the poster's answer is that the window has more handles on it than the two binding arms: the affinities, geometries and epitopes of both arms, certainly, but also novel CD3 binders, binders against the gamma-delta (γδ) T-cell receptor, and costimulatory binders against CD28 and 4-1BB. B7-H4- and PSMA-targeted engagers built from these combinations differ from the clinical benchmarks in paired killing and cytokine readouts, measured as T-cell-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (TDCC) with human pan-T cells for 72 hours. Where two PSMA arms behaved differently, cryo-EM of the Fab–PSMA complexes said why: both bind close to the membrane, but in distinct orientations, one pointing down and one up.

The other three levers each come with a panel behind them, and each is the same move as the CD3 one. Of 184 γδ1 and γδ2 antibodies assessed for binding, 71 γδ1 and 31 γδ2 binders were cross-reactive with cyno, and clustering their competition data resolved the γδ2 set into 17 epitope communities; recruiting γδ T cells selectively, the poster argues, could reduce the risk of cytokine release syndrome (CRS). The CD28 binders fall into 7 epitope communities and behave as conditional agonists, active only where FcγRIIb is present, with no superagonist activity against the TGN1412 (theralizumab) control the assay includes for that purpose. The 4-1BB binders come in 12 ligand-blocking and 44 non-blocking flavours, in 6 epitope communities, with no FcγRIIb-independent activity.

Read against the earlier pages here, this looks like a change of field, and it is less of one than it appears. Profile a large panel on what its members do, find the axes that actually organise it, then select along those axes instead of picking favourites: that is the move in [the carbon potentials article →](/work/carbon/), where the panel was a training set and the axes were structural, and it is the move here, where the panel is a portfolio of antibodies and the axes are killing and cytokine release. The units change and the failure modes do not. A cheap score that is confidently wrong somewhere nobody has looked costs the same in either field, except that here the thing you did not look at is a peptide on a healthy cell.
